 unpersuaded. The law relating to the registration of an FIR by issuing
 directions under Section 156(3) of the CrPC is well settled. Indeed, the said
 provision empowers the Magistrate to direct the police to conduct an
 investigation in cases where it is found that the police have either failed to
 investigate the matter or have not conducted the investigation satisfactorily.
 Such investigation may also be monitored by the Magistrate. 12. In Sukwasi v. State of Uttar Pradesh7 , the Court observed that
 applications under Section 156(3) of CrPC, are being filed in large numbers.
 The Court emphasized that the provision should be used sparingly and only
 in unusual or extraordinary situations, where there is a miscarriage of justice
 warranting a direction to the police to register a case. The Court further
 noted that since an alternative remedy of filing a complaint under Section
 200 of CrPC exists, recourse to Section 156(3) should not ordinarily be
 permitted. It was clarified that there is no legal mandate requiring a
 Magistrate to allow an application under Section 156(3) CrPC; the
 Magistrate retains the discretion to allow or reject such an application. 13. This position was reaffirmed in Ravindra Kumar v. State (Govt. of
 NCT of Delhi) & Anr.8 , where the Court reiterated that Section 156(3) CrPC
 is a discretionary provision. The use of the word "may" indicates that the 6 2014 (2) SCC 1. 2008 Crl LJ 472. This is a digitally signed order. The authenticity of the order can be re-verified from Delhi High Court Order Portal by scanning the QR code shown above.

 Magistrate is not bound to refer every complaint to the police for
 investigation. The Court stressed that the Magistrate must apply his judicial
 mind and may direct investigation only when satisfied that the complaint
 discloses cognizable offences and that a police investigation is necessary to
 gather evidence that the complainant cannot obtain independently. Thus, a
 complainant does not enjoy an unfettered right to seek a direction under Section 156(3) in every case. 14. The Supreme Court, in Sakiri Vasu v. State of Uttar Pradesh &
 Ors.9 , further clarified the procedural safeguards available to an aggrieved
 person in such cases. It held that where an FIR is not registered by the
 concerned police station, the first remedy lies in approaching the
 Superintendent of Police under Section 154(3) of CrPC or any another
 competent officer under Section 36 of CrPC. If the grievance remains
 unresolved, only then may the person approach the Magistrate under Section
 156(3) of CrPC, instead of immediately resorting to a writ petition or
 invoking the High Court's inherent jurisdiction under Section 482 of CrPC.
 The Court also emphasized that the complainant continues to have the
 alternative remedy of filing a private complaint under Section 200 of CrPC,
 thereby reinforcing the view that recourse to Section 156(3) must be
 exercised judiciously and as a matter of exception rather than rule. 15. In light of the foregoing legal principles, which have been duly

 exercise its powers under Section 156(3) of CrPC, such power must not be
 invoked mechanically at the mere instance of the complainant. These powers
 are to be exercised primarily in cases involving serious allegations where the
 evidence is beyond the reach of the complainant, or where custodial
 interrogation is necessary for recovery of an article or discovery of facts.
 Such circumstances are not discernible in the facts of the present case. 18. In light of the above observations, this Court finds no reason to
 invoke its inherent jurisdiction under Section 528 of BNSS (corresponding
 to Section 482 of CrPC) to interfere with the revisional order passed by the
 Sessions Court. 19. Accordingly, the present petition is dismissed along with pending
