The Supreme Court has asked banks to actively step in and alert customers when they make unusually large withdrawals, stressing that financial institutions must not remain passive while “digital arrest” scams drain victims of their savings and must put systems in place to flag and halt suspicious transactions.

The court said that if a pensioner who usually withdraws Rs 10,000-20,000 ends up transferring Rs 25-50 lakh, such transactions should immediately trigger an alert, according to a LiveLaw report.

The observations were made in a suo motu case initiated by the Supreme Court to address the rising incidence of digital arrest scams and their prevalence in the country. A bench comprising Chief Justice of India Surya Kant, Justice Joymalya Bagchi and Justice N.V. Anjaria took up the matter amid concerns over the misuse of banking channels by fraudsters impersonating law-enforcement officials.

During the hearing, Attorney General for India R. Venkataramani informed the court that the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has drafted a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for banks, including provisions that allow temporary debit holds to prevent fraudulent transactions. Acting on this submission, the court directed the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA) to formally adopt the SOP and issue directions for its implementation nationwide.

Furthermore, the Chief Justice questioned why banking systems fail to detect obvious anomalies, asking, “why your AI operated tools in the bank did not deem it fit to alarm him, that this transaction is suspicious?”

Beyond transaction monitoring, the Supreme Court strongly criticised what it described as a broader failure by banks to recognise their public responsibility in the face of large-scale cyber fraud. Emphasising that profit motives cannot override depositor trust, the bench reminded banks of their fiduciary role. “In the over-anxiety of making profits, banks must realise they are trustees of public money. People have deposited because they trust the banks,” the Chief Justice said, warning that banks were increasingly becoming a liability for the public.

The court also flagged systemic risks arising from banks prioritising business interests over safeguards. Justice Joymalya Bagchi observed that banks were operating “more into business mode” and cautioned that, in doing so, they were becoming “either innocently or connivingly, platforms through which there is a swift and seamless transmission of stolen proceeds of crime.” He pointed to data from the MHA showing that more than Rs 52,000 crore had been misappropriated through cyber fraud between April 2021 and November 2025.

Meanwhile, the bench signalled that regulatory inaction could invite judicial intervention. “We hope you don’t invite our directions,” the Chief Justice remarked, stressing that defining suspicious transactions should be the primary consideration for banks and asked the RBI to introduce a mechanism.

The Union government told the Supreme Court that SIM-binding for messaging platforms like WhatsApp could help curb digital arrest scams by making it harder for fraudsters to operate anonymously over over-the-top (OTT) channels. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology and the Department of Telecommunications (DoT) said they have issued directions to implement SIM-binding, which links a user’s messaging account more tightly to a verified mobile number.

Officials said an inter-departmental committee has met twice and focused on systemic vulnerabilities in SIM issuance and management, which scammers exploit to mask their identities and locations. It was noted that misuse of OTT platforms, particularly WhatsApp, has been a major conduit for digital arrest frauds, and that SIM-binding could reduce that misuse by restricting access to verified SIM-linked accounts.

The committee also discussed rules under the Telecommunications Act, 2023, that would allow biometric verification in SIM issuance and emphasised that technological controls such as blocking spoofed calls have already reduced harmful traffic through the Central International Out Roamer (CIOR) mechanism, which blocked millions of suspicious calls.

On December 1, 2025, the Supreme Court directed the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) to lead a pan-India probe into “digital arrest” scams during the same case. The court said “digital arrest scams require immediate attention of the premier investigating agency of the country,” and authorised the CBI to investigate not just the crimes themselves but also the role of banking institutions that may have enabled such operations, including probing bankers under the Prevention of Corruption Act where necessary and seeking Interpol cooperation on cross-border elements.

Furthermore, in March 2025, the MHA reported blocking over 83,000 WhatsApp accounts and nearly 4,000 Skype IDs linked to these schemes, along with 7.81 lakh SIM cards and over 2 lakh International Mobile Equipment Identity (IMEI) numbers.

Digital arrest scams have rapidly evolved into a significant cybercrime problem, cutting across regions and leaving victims with severe financial losses. Cases show how fraudsters impersonate police or judicial authorities to intimidate targets into transferring money, often within hours, by exploiting fear of legal action.

In a case reported today , a 68-year-old retiree was duped of nearly Rs 25 lakh after being subjected to sustained pressure by callers posing as cybercrime and Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) officials, illustrating how these scams combine social engineering with psychological coercion.

This trend matters because it highlights systemic challenges: banks and payment systems currently lack uniformly robust real-time fraud detection, telecom issuance remains vulnerable to SIM-based exploits, and victims often report to authorities only after irreversible transfers.

Furthermore, these frauds exploit the very technologies – mobile phones, messaging apps and online banking – that have democratised financial access. Without robust detection and prevention systems, innocent users face real financial, emotional and legal harm. Moreover, the involvement of mule accounts, SIM misuse and cross-jurisdictional networks complicates enforcement and recovery efforts, placing strain on state cyber units and national coordination mechanisms.
