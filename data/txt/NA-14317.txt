Iran’s nationwide internet shutdown has crossed 84 hours, cutting off most of the country from the outside world as authorities intensified a crackdown on widespread anti-government protests. Internet monitoring group NetBlocks said the blackout, imposed on January 8, continues across Iran with only limited and risky workarounds available.

“As Iran wakes up to a new day, metrics show the national internet blackout is past the 84-hour mark,” NetBlocks said, adding that access is possible only through alternatives such as shortwave radio, border-area mobile signals, Starlink, and satellite phones.

The prolonged shutdown has raised serious concerns among human rights groups and digital rights experts, who warn that it is being used to hide the scale of violence against protesters. Amnesty International said the blackout itself amounts to a human rights violation and prevents documentation of killings and arrests.

“The Iranian authorities have once again deliberately blocked internet access inside Iran to hide the true extent of the grave human rights violations and crimes under international law they are carrying out to crush the largest nationwide protests,” said Rebecca White, researcher at Amnesty International’s Security Lab.

Meanwhile, the Human Rights Activists News Agency reports that security forces have killed at least 544 people and arrested over 10,681 during the protests. Protesters have demonstrated at 585 locations across 186 cities in all 31 provinces. Amnesty International has independently confirmed the killing of at least 28 protesters and bystanders, including children, between December 31 and January 3, and says it is investigating reports of further deaths after the internet shutdown.

Despite the blackout, videos continue to emerge through satellite connections. Journalist and activist Masih Alinejad shared footage from Tehran, saying, “Videos received from Tehran via Starlink: Despite mass killings and widespread repression, people are still in the streets.”

The protests, which began on December 28, were initially triggered by a sharp currency collapse, soaring inflation, and worsening economic conditions. They have since expanded into broader demonstrations calling for the end of the Islamic Republic system and opposing Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s leadership.

Exiled former Crown Prince Reza Pahlavi has openly backed the protesters and called for an escalation of the movement. In a video message posted on X, he said : “I announce another stage of the national uprising to overthrow the Islamic Republic and reclaim our dear Iran.”

He also urged Iranians working in state institutions and security forces to side with protesters and called government propaganda and communication infrastructure “legitimate targets”. Addressing Iranians abroad, he said embassies should fly Iran’s national flag instead of that of the Islamic Republic.

International concern has also grown. US President Donald Trump said Iran appears to be nearing a “red line” set by his administration over the treatment of protesters. Speaking to reporters aboard Air Force One, he said: “They’re starting to, it looks like.”

Trump added that the US was reviewing its options, stating, “We’re looking at some very strong options; we’ll make a determination.” He did not specify what actions Washington might take, though US media reports say he has been briefed on possible military responses.

During the November 2019 protests and the 2022 Woman, Life, Freedom uprising, authorities imposed similar blackouts, and investigators later confirmed hundreds of deaths once connectivity returned.

Human rights groups warn that as long as the blackout continues, the true scale of arrests, injuries, and killings may remain unknown. Amnesty International has called on Iranian authorities to immediately restore full internet access and urged the international community to take urgent diplomatic action to prevent further bloodshed.

Across the world, governments increasingly use internet shutdowns and restrictions to curb dissent and control information during political unrest, protests, elections, or security crises.

In 2024, digital rights groups documented 296 internet shutdowns across 54 countries , many of them imposed during protests, conflict, and civil unrest, showing that governments increasingly use connectivity cuts as a tool of control rather than as technical or administrative measures. Countries that have frequently resorted to shutdowns include Myanmar , where the military imposed hundreds of blackouts after the 2021 coup; India , which recorded one of the highest numbers of shutdowns globally; Pakistan, which imposed repeated forced outages; and Russia , where authorities linked shutdowns to conflict zones and tighter regulation of digital platforms.

In Africa , nations such as Ethiopia and Sudan have experienced repeated shutdowns, particularly linked to regional conflicts and political instability. Reports also show shutdowns or large-scale restrictions in Bangladesh and other regions during protests or political tensions. Beyond shutdowns, some countries , including China, North Korea, and Turkmenistan, maintain long-term internet censorship and platform bans that limit open access to global networks.

In many cases, authorities have justified the measures on grounds of public order, national security, or preventing misinformation. However, rights groups argue that the practical effect is often the same: muting dissent, disrupting independent reporting, and preventing citizens from communicating with each other and the outside world during critical moments. Global data shows that the use of internet shutdowns has risen sharply over the past decade, indicating that connectivity itself has become a key pressure point in modern political crises.
