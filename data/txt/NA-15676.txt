The Delhi High Court recently ordered Google to remove a video by YouTuber Shyam Meera Singh titled ‘Sadhguru EXPOSED: What’s Happening in Jaggi Vasudev’s Ashram?’, related to the Isha Foundation and its founder, Jaggi Vasudev, commonly known as Sadhguru.

Moreover, the court ordered that “Any member of the public is also restrained from uploading the very same video on any social media platforms till the next date of hearing,” also restricting Singh from further publishing or sharing the video.

“Continuous circulation of the video is likely to cause harm to the Plaintiff/Trust’s reputation and as stated that more than 09 lakhs views and more than 13500 comments have already been reported. If the video is not taken down immediately, the Plaintiff/Trust will suffer loss of reputation which cannot be compensated monetarily.”

Isha Foundation argued in court that the content in question damages its reputation and is defamatory in nature, directly harming how the public perceives the organisation. The foundation stated that it is “dedicated to raising human consciousness and fostering global harmony through individual transformation” and such allegations tarnish its reputation.

The Delhi HC noted that the video clip portrays Isha Foundation as engaging in “practices not accepted in society” while agreeing with the foundation’s arguments. However, the concept of “reputation” remains ambiguous—perhaps even dangerously so—as authorities often invoke it to justify restrictions on human rights and freedom of speech. Some argue that a more balanced approach would be to tie “reputation” to human dignity, ensuring that its protection does not come at the cost of fundamental freedoms.

In a 2016 ruling, the Supreme Court (SC) upheld the legality of criminal defamation, stating that it does not impose an excessive restriction on free speech, as safeguarding one’s reputation is both a fundamental and human right.

In a separate ruling on sexual harassment and defamation, a Delhi court acquitted journalist Priya Ramani in a case filed by former Union Minister MJ Akbar. The court asserted that safeguarding reputation should not come at the expense of dignity and upheld the defence of “truth in public interest.”

However, distinguishing between public interest and what simply attracts public attention remains a challenge, as the two are not always the same. Just because something garners attention doesn’t necessarily mean it serves the public good.

This isn’t the first time Isha Foundation—and by extension, Sadhguru—have come under public scrutiny or taken legal action in response. Previously, a couple who volunteered at the foundation alleged that it was unsafe for children, claiming that its educational institutions frequently enabled child sexual abuse, with management deliberately suppressing multiple cases.

Following these allegations, Isha Foundation filed a defamation lawsuit against Tamil news outlet Nakkheeran, seeking the removal of YouTube videos and demanding Rs. 3 crore in damages. Nakkheeran had published a series of videos discussing the various allegations against Isha Foundation related to the mistreatment of children.

Just as in the present case, the foundation named YouTube and Google as respondents, highlighting a broader pattern of legal action against platforms and individuals hosting critical content.

The inclusion of Google as a respondent in these lawsuits is particularly interesting, given that under India’s Information Technology (IT) (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules , 2021, it qualifies as an intermediary rather than a direct content publisher.

Despite this, plaintiffs frequently name platforms like YouTube as defendants in defamation and content-related lawsuits, raising questions about the scope and limitations of intermediary liability in India.

Previously, Google CEO Sundar Pichai faced a contempt notice from a Mumbai court over YouTube’s non-compliance with a previous Bombay High Court order to remove a video alleged to be defamatory. The court observed that, although the platform removed the video in India, users outside the country could still access it, sparking judicial frustration over its selective enforcement of takedown orders. This case highlights the growing tension between platforms leveraging safe harbour protections and courts demanding stricter compliance with content regulation.

Similarly, news agency ANI has taken Wikipedia to court for defamation, further highlighting how legal battles over content increasingly involve digital platforms, despite their intermediary status. These cases signal a shifting legal landscape, where platforms once considered neutral hosts of content are now facing heightened scrutiny and pressure to proactively regulate what appears on their sites.

While India’s safe harbour provisions under Section 79 of the IT Act provide intermediaries legal protection from liability for third-party content, this protection is not absolute. Authorities require platforms to comply with content takedown orders, and as long as they follow these directives, they generally avoid further legal consequences.

Recently, a petition by Software Freedom Law Center filed in the SC also highlighted that intermediaries protected under Safe Harbour provisions have no choice but to comply with the Centre’s directions to continue operations, leaving the user as the only aggrieved party, unable to present their defence.

This framework allows platforms like YouTube to comply with orders when necessary while largely avoiding direct legal accountability for user-generated content.
