The Indian Film and Television Producers Council’s (IFTPC) framing of negative reviews as “extortion” is significant because it shifts the problem from disclosure and transparency into the realm of criminal law. India already has the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) guidelines to regulate paid promotions. Influencers must disclose material connections so audiences know when a review is an advertisement. That framework is about commercial speech.

By contrast, calling negative reviews “extortion” risks criminalising opinion. Moreover, precedent matters here. Platforms like Zomato in India, and Rotten Tomatoes or IMDb globally, have treated review manipulation as a moderation challenge. They built tools and adjusted weighting systems instead of resorting to legal threats against reviewers.

What makes the IFTPC’s stance even more problematic is the grey zone it creates. For example, what if a movie is genuinely bad and a critic says so? How do you distinguish honest criticism from what a producer claims is sabotage? If every harsh review risks being labelled “extortion,” critics may self-censor. As a result, audiences lose the benefit of candid feedback. Using defamation or extortion law in this way risks chilling criticism and undermining free speech.

The IFTPC has announced that it will consult legal counsel to explore civil and criminal remedies against influencers accused of posting malicious negative reviews. The producers’ body, which represents more than 375 members, says it has received complaints that some influencers are deliberately undermining films or using the threat of poor reviews to extract benefits.

It described these actions as “acts of extortion” that go beyond fair criticism.

In its press release dated September 1, 2025, the IFTPC said it has observed an “alarming trend” of certain social media influencers engaging in “predatory practices.” According to the Council, these influencers release “malicious and disparaging reviews or reaction videos of films, series, and other audiovisual content, often coupled with demands for payment from producers.”

The letter claims that when producers refuse such demands, influencers launch targeted campaigns to deliberately sabotage a project’s reception and commercial viability.

The IFTPC stressed that it is “absolutely not against the freedom of speech” and welcomes “genuine, constructive criticism.” However, it argued that the “acts of extortion” by some unscrupulous individuals “go far beyond the purview of legitimate reviewing” and are a “grave threat to the creative and economic health of the Indian film and entertainment industry.”

To address this, the Council said it has decided to seek opinions from “top-tier legal counsels” and will “explore all available legal remedies under both civil and criminal laws to bring a swift and decisive end to these extortionate practices.”

ASCI already regulates influencer marketing. Its guidelines require influencers to disclose material connections such as payments, gifts, discounts, or even free stays.

The goal is to ensure transparency so that consumers know when they are engaging with advertising. Importantly, these rules do not treat reviews or opinions as criminal conduct. They only ask influencers to disclose commercial relationships.

This approach highlights a contrast. While ASCI focuses on disclosure and transparency, the IFTPC has framed negative reviews as potential “extortion.” Therefore, the debate shifts into criminal law rather than remaining within industry self-regulation.

The challenge of malicious reviews is not new in India. Back In 2017, Zomato acknowledged that some users were blackmailing restaurants by demanding free meals or discounts in exchange for positive ratings. At the same time, some restaurants were bribing customers with perks to post favourable reviews.

Instead of turning to legal remedies, the food delivery giant responded by designing tools and policies. It introduced a “report abuse” feature that allowed restaurants to flag reviews they believed were posted with bad intent. It also created systems to freeze suspicious user accounts and delete their past reviews. Moreover, the platform promised to shame restaurants that engaged in bribery by publicly marking them.

Zomato’s history also shows how public outrage can distort review systems. After the Mocambo incident in Kolkata, where a restaurant was accused of discriminatory behaviour, thousands of negative reviews flooded the page in a single day. Zomato responded by pausing its spam filters and flagging the incident for moderation.

Earlier, the viral Lemp Brewpub controversy in Gurugram had already led Zomato to introduce a system where new users had to publish at least ten reviews and have their accounts approved before their posts became visible.

These cases demonstrate something important. Indian platforms have treated review manipulation as a moderation problem to be solved through product changes, not as an issue requiring criminal complaints.

Elsewhere, international platforms have also struggled with review bombing. IMDb makes it clear that it cannot verify whether every user has watched a film before rating it.

Instead, it uses a weighted average that applies safeguards against ballot stuffing. While all ratings are visible in the breakdown, the displayed score reflects adjustments made to counter manipulation. The system relies on trust, honesty, and algorithmic detection.

Moreover, IMDb openly admits that some users try to inflate or deflate ratings but treats it as a matter of product integrity, not a legal violation.

Rotten Tomatoes has faced similar challenges and has responded more aggressively. It separates critic reviews, which generate the Tomatometer score, from user ratings, which create the Popcornmeter.

After repeated review bombing incidents, Rotten Tomatoes blocked pre-release audience reviews. It also introduced “Verified Reviews” in 2019 and linked them directly to ticket purchases through Fandango, its parent company, which sells movie tickets and provides film and television information to millions of users. Verified reviews carry a clear label, while unverified reviews still appear but remain distinguished.

This system ensures that at least some audience reviews connect back to actual ticket buyers. In both cases, the pattern is clear: platforms treat review manipulation as a design and moderation problem. They address it through weighting, verification, and disclosure. They do not threaten users with criminal action.

Indian law does provide remedies, but the threshold stands far higher than the IFTPC suggests. The law defines extortion as a situation where someone intentionally puts another in fear of injury and then dishonestly induces that person to hand over money, property, or something of value.

Classic examples include a threat to publish defamatory material unless money is paid, or forcing someone to sign a bond under threat of violence. A film review, however harsh, does not reach this threshold unless the reviewer directly links a demand to a threat. Without proof of such a demand, calling a negative review “extortion” does not align with the law.

Defamation is broader but still limited by explicit exceptions. The law says it is defamation to make or publish imputations that harm reputation. However, it lists ten exceptions that protect free expression.

The most important one here is the sixth exception. It states that it is not defamation to give, in good faith, an opinion about the merits of a public performance.

Once a film is released, it is considered submitted to the judgment of the public. This means that reviews, whether positive or negative, are legally protected as long as they are made in good faith.

Courts have echoed this principle. The Kerala High Court (HC) explored whether guidelines were needed to regulate influencer-driven film reviews, focusing on paid or fake negative reviews and disclosure obligations. The aim was to address “review bombing” without directly restricting legitimate criticism.

The Madras HC went further, rejecting a plea to block reviews in the first three days after release. It held that such a restriction would be an unreasonable curb on speech.

This debate is bigger than a few angry reviews. If producers succeed in framing online criticism as a criminal act, it could set a precedent that other industries use to silence users and influencers. Every sector that deals with reviews, from restaurants to e-commerce to gaming, could point to this as justification for threatening critics with defamation or extortion charges.

Moreover, platforms already face pressure to moderate reviews for bias and manipulation. If courts entertain criminal complaints, they may over-police speech, take down critical voices, or build costly verification systems just to shield themselves.

For audiences, the risk is clear. If critics and influencers self-censor out of fear of lawsuits or police action, viewers lose access to independent opinion. What remains are only promotional voices or “approved” reviews. That would erode trust in the entire ecosystem of online recommendation and debate.

The debate around influencer accountability is unfolding against a wider regulatory backdrop . India already has ASCI’s disclosure rules, Securities and Exchange Board of India’s (SEBI) finfluencer crackdown, and Consumer Affairs’ “endorsement know-hows.” On top of that, in April 2025 the Indian Influencer Governance Council (IIGC), a self-regulatory body, released a comprehensive Code of Standards for influencer content. If film reviews are folded into this trend, the line between regulating transparency and policing criticism could blur even further.

In short, what looks like a fight between producers and influencers is really a test case for how India handles digital criticism. The outcome could redefine the boundary between moderation, regulation, and censorship.
