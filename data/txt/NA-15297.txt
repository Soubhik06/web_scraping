The government has stepped up efforts to tackle “ dark patterns ” — manipulative design tactics that steer users into unintended choices, such as hidden fees, forced subscriptions, or fake urgency prompts.

In response to growing consumer complaints, the Department of Consumer Affairs has set up a 19-member Joint Working Group (JWG) to address the issue. The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) has also asked platforms to act swiftly and audit their interfaces in an advisory.

On May 28 , Union Consumer Affairs Minister Pralhad Joshi met representatives from Amazon, Swiggy, Zomato, Flipkart, MakeMyTrip, Meta, WhatsApp, Apple, and others in New Delhi. He asked these companies to stop using dark patterns and follow the CCPA guidelines.

The CCPA had released guidelines in November 2023 identifying 13 types of dark patterns like drip pricing, basket sneaking, and confirm shaming. However, it removed Clause 8 from the notified version. This clause, which had appeared in the draft version, had stated that the provisions of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, would apply in case of any guideline violations.

On June 5 , the Department of Consumer Affairs issued a memo forming the Joint Working Group. The group will meet regularly over the next 12 months and submit reports to the government.

The group will flag violations to the Department of Consumer Affairs, recommend user safeguards, and suggest awareness campaigns.

The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) issued an advisory dated June 5, 2025, under Section 18 (1) of the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 , asking e-commerce platforms to conduct internal audits to detect dark patterns. The advisory directs platforms to complete these audits within three months and references the Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023.

It also cites Rule 4(9) of the E-Commerce Rules, 2020 , which requires that consent must be obtained through clear and affirmative user action. Additionally, the advisory encourages e-commerce platforms to submit self-declarations affirming that they are not indulging in any dark patterns.

The government is stepping up. With a task force in place and a call for self-audits, it’s showing that it wants platforms to take dark patterns seriously.

Still, the outcome depends on how platforms respond. The working group can give advice, but it can’t enforce anything. Since the advisory has no penalties, companies aren’t under real pressure to act. The task force brings in much-needed expertise, but without legal teeth, these efforts may do little to change how platforms operate.
