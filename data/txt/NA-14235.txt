About two years ago, a young girl reached out to me, hesitantly and afraid I’d judge her. She confessed that one of her private videos had leaked due to a bug on a social media platform and had spread across the internet, appearing on all social media platforms, the dark web, and porn sites. My first instinct was to tell her why she recorded the video and that she should know phones can get hacked. However, I resisted, as I could see she was on the verge of a mental breakdown. She feared her conservative parents might find the video on their phones and shame her.

She was reluctant to seek police help, but I coaxed her into it and ensured her case would be handled with the privacy and sensitivity it needed. However, as the video went viral, I reached out to a few platforms for help. They pitched in, but since the video had leaked widely, it was difficult to trace its origins and remove it completely from the internet. There were no consequences; the platforms shrugged off their responsibility and moved on. The police faced challenges in holding anyone accountable. The culprits and the platforms got away while the young girl was left to face the consequences. I felt helpless and as if I had failed to ensure she received justice.

This situation reminded me of the Bulli Bai and Sulli Deals issues, which I took up with the Mumbai Police. They managed to hunt the culprits down, but legal provisions ensured they received bail. A deterrent was set: when using the internet, just like in civil society, individuals should face action if they breach the law. This will become increasingly relevant as more people move toward digital societies.

If we aim to build a creator economy, we must understand this basic rule. Recently, when the controversy surrounding India’s Got Latent broke out, I raised questions about the boundaries of freedom of speech and whether content creators should take responsibility for what they publish. This also raised concerns about the cascading effects these influencers have not just on society but also on young, impressionable minds who may believe that quick fame and recognition come from pushing the boundaries of freedom of speech, along with the ethical responsibilities of content creation.

Of course, I am not in favor of any censorship or laws that interfere in the creative realm. However, when content creators do not self-regulate, they leave the space open for state intervention. I mentioned that I would raise this in the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Communications and Information Technology, of which I am a member, alongside 31 other honorable MPs across political party lines. This led to much blame and accusations against me. I was reminded of more pressing issues in the country, and I urge those critics to check my parliamentary record before telling me what I should be concerned about.

There are multiple layers to this need for intervention. Freedom of speech is not black and white; it has always been a nuanced topic. I recognize the complex evolution of freedom of speech in India, where we have evolved with certain restrictions while being mindful of the country’s cultural context. When content is made available on public platforms with minimal age filters, creators have a duty to consider the impact their words and actions can have on different groups in society.

The Indian Constitution does give every citizen the right to freedom of speech. Article 19(1)(a) states that all citizens shall have the right to freedom of speech and expression. I read a recent article in MediaNama by Nikhil Pahwa, whom I respect for the wisdom he brings to debates on internet policies and navigating digital growth. He wrote, and I quote:

“MP Priyanka Chaturvedi surprised me by stating that she will raise the issue of online content with the Parliamentary Standing Committee on IT. Chaturvedi, who has in the past defended free speech, is probably reading the room, following the mob, and doing what politicians do.”

I continue to defend freedom of speech and advocate for minimal state intervention; however, I refuse to live in a created silo of ‘liberal gatekeepers’ and ‘conservative gatekeepers’ of freedom of speech. Most countries, including the UK and Australia, have devised ways to address the challenge of balancing freedom of speech while limiting harmful digital content by setting guidelines in place. This is not mob mentality but the existing reality we cannot escape.

He further states, “The political one-upmanship to appeal to conservative views on issues should not come at the cost of individual and constitutional rights. What we are witnessing is not just public backlash—it is a coordinated attempt to roll back hard-won freedoms under the guise of cultural preservation. Chaturvedi should know better, and of course, someone on the Internet was quick to point out her inconsistency.”

Let me reiterate to my dear friend that our constitutional right under Article 19(1) comes with the constitutional responsibility outlined in Article 19(2). I fail to understand how my stance advocating for broader public consultation can lead to a rollback of what the Constitution mandates for us. Moreover, this issue is not isolated; it has given rise to various content in multiple languages across regions, where budding content creators feel that using abusive language, mocking serious health challenges, making rape jokes, and promoting incest is the quickest way to gain attention, subscribers, and financial success.

So, if you own content to gain fame, own it to make money, own it as a career, and own it to become an influencer, then why can’t you own it when it leads to consequences? Regarding the second part of his sentence about my inconsistency, yes, a decade ago I spoke my mind as an individual with a large following on X (formerly Twitter) during the AIB controversy. However, I now speak as a legislator, not just as an individual, but as a voice for various sections of society who have expressed their concerns.

I would urge my friend to look at Instagram reels or any platform of his choice where parents use their minor children to gain fame. Mothers, in a bid to join the creator economy, are making sensual reels with their sons, and parents are sexualizing their minor daughters. Minor girls are exposing themselves for quick fame, and soft porn is readily available!

While Nikhil states the principle of “don’t like, don’t watch,” he forgets that the algorithms on these platforms push viral content to users’ screens, regardless of whether it is appropriate for adults or minors. This lures viewers into watching, leading to more money for creators and profits for the platforms. Even if some content is behind paywalls, snippets are often shared widely on public platforms, exacerbating the issue.This issue is not politically motivated for me.

While BJP-ruled states like Maharashtra, Rajasthan, and Assam have filed FIRs against Allahbadia and other comedians on the panel, seemingly racing to hold them accountable, my intention in raising this matter in the Parliamentary Committee is to start a meaningful conversation about ensuring measures to address these concerns. There is a loophole that has led to multiple FIRs, with the state coming down strongly against these creators and raising disproportionate outrage and questions regarding OTT and intermediary platforms. My intent was to address these critical gaps in the system.

This case has been in the spotlight, and those responsible are facing considerable cultural and legal backlash. However, this is not an isolated offense, nor is Allahbadia the sole offender.

At the end of the day, this isn’t just about one show or one comedian. This is a larger conversation about how comedians and content creators exploit freedom of speech without considering the consequences. As politicians, it is not our job to fuel outrage or stir controversy, but it is our responsibility to identify the gaps in our system that allow incidents like this to occur. We need to raise awareness about these issues so we can prevent harm to society and create a more responsible, respectful space for free speech.

Priyanka Chaturvedi is a Member of Parliament.
