The January 7 memorandum does not pull the United States out of the internet or dismantle a binding cyber treaty. It pulls the country out of the institutional spaces where the rules of the internet are shaped, normalised, and carried across borders.

Cybersecurity and digital governance rarely emerge from a single agreement. They develop through sustained participation in multilateral forums that define responsible state behaviour online, coordinate responses to online extremism, influence how platforms address digital harms, and shape norms around data flows and digital trade. These forums generate soft law, shared practices, and technical consensus that often harden into regulation and international standards over time.

When a major power exits these spaces, governance does not stall. The rules continue to evolve, but without that actor’s influence. Agenda-setting shifts to those that remain, and the norms that take hold increasingly reflect their priorities.

This is where the withdrawal carries weight. As the US steps back from broad-based multilateral institutions, digital governance does not become lighter or less restrictive. It becomes more fragmented, more bloc-driven, and more reliant on bilateral leverage than shared processes.

For countries like India, this narrows strategic room for manoeuvre, forcing choices between competing digital governance models rather than shaping a common framework, whether that is the European Union’s regulation-heavy approach, China’s state-centric model of digital sovereignty, or US-led market-driven norms that now lack a multilateral anchor.

On January 7, the United States issued a presidential memorandum directing federal agencies to withdraw from dozens of international organisations and United Nations bodies that it considers “contrary to the interests of the United States.”

The list spans both UN and non-UN entities and cuts across climate, development, democracy, security, and governance. While the memorandum does not explicitly target cyber policy or digital regulation, several of the bodies named play a direct or indirect role in shaping global norms on cybersecurity, internet governance, online safety, and digital cooperation.

The order instructs agencies to take immediate steps to withdraw from or cease participation in these organisations, including halting funding where permitted by law. It also notes that the review of additional organisations and conventions remains ongoing.

Several of the organisations the US plans to exit operate at the intersection of technology, governance, and security, even if they are not formally labelled as cyber bodies.

The January 7 memorandum reflects growing scepticism within US domestic politics toward multilateral institutions that shape norms through consensus rather than deliver direct strategic or economic returns. Across party lines, there has been a stronger push for sovereignty-first policymaking and a preference for bilateral or minilateral arrangements where US leverage is clearer and outcomes are easier to control.

This approach is not entirely new. Earlier withdrawals from forums such as the Paris Agreement and the World Health Organisation were driven by similar concerns about cost, autonomy, and institutional overreach. What distinguishes the current move is its focus on quieter norm-setting bodies rather than headline treaties, allowing the US to limit multilateral exposure without immediate legal or economic fallout even as those institutions continue to shape global digital rules.

Whether this signals a broader reorientation of US engagement with global governance, or a tactical recalibration focused on specific institutions, remains unclear. But the scope and timing of the withdrawals suggest a deliberate shift away from consensus-driven norm-building toward forms of engagement where control and leverage are more directly exercised.

Policy analysts have warned that US withdrawal from multilateral cyber forums creates an immediate leadership gap. Research from the Atlantic Council has documented how China and Russia actively use UN bodies and technical standard-setting organisations to promote a model of digital sovereignty centred on state control and surveillance.

Without sustained US participation, forums that previously reinforced open and interoperable internet norms risk tilting toward more restrictive approaches. Analysts writing in Tech Policy Press argue that this erosion is likely to be incremental rather than dramatic, but that over time it reshapes consensus around what constitutes responsible state behaviour in cyberspace.

The move also strains trust among US allies that aligned their digital policies with US-backed norms. Long-standing cyber partners lose a key participant in multilateral negotiations and begin to question Washington’s reliability as a norm-setting partner.

This concern has already surfaced in digital trade debates. Analysts at the Center for European Policy Analysis have pointed to criticism from US Senator Ron Wyden, who described recent US reversals on digital trade commitments as “a win for China,” warning that such shifts undermine allied coordination and weaken collective bargaining power.

The effects extend to smaller groupings such as the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue, which brings together the US, India, Japan, and Australia.

Research from Perry World House suggests the Quad already faces challenges sustaining coherent cyber and technology initiatives without consistent US leadership.

Furthermore, analysts at the Stimson Center have noted that divergent cyber approaches among Quad members complicate coordination. A broader US retreat from multilateral norm-building risks further slowing progress on shared cybersecurity, digital infrastructure, and technology standards initiatives.

The US has historically funded cybersecurity training, incident-response coordination, and online safety programmes across regions. Tech Policy Press has documented how earlier inward-focused policy phases led to the dismantling of several internet freedom and global cyber cooperation initiatives.

A renewed withdrawal reduces available resources for developing countries to strengthen cyber resilience and pushes civil society organisations working on online safety, digital rights, and internet freedom to seek alternative sources of support.

US disengagement also injects uncertainty into global digital trade discussions. Analysts point to the postponement of digital trade talks under the Indo-Pacific Economic Framework as an early indicator of how multilateral coordination on data governance can stall without sustained US advocacy.

Without a strong US push, efforts at the World Trade Organization to limit data localisation requirements and promote cross-border data flows may lose momentum, increasing regulatory fragmentation and compliance costs for businesses operating across jurisdictions.

If the US exit from multilateral digital forums persists, the consequences extend beyond short-term coordination gaps to the structure of global digital governance itself.

Over time, sustained absence produces a different effect. Cyber and digital governance evolve through consistent engagement and agenda-setting across institutions. Norms harden into standards because actors consistently show up, shape agendas, and normalise certain positions across institutions.

As mentioned earlier, research from the Atlantic Council highlights how China and Russia have invested in long-term norm-setting strategies within bodies such as the International Telecommunication Union and UN cybercrime negotiations to advance a model of digital sovereignty centred on state control and surveillance. If the US remains disengaged, resistance to these approaches erodes not through confrontation, but through normalisation.

In that scenario, the central question shifts. It is no longer whether cyber rules will emerge, but whose priorities will define what becomes accepted as legitimate state behaviour online.

A prolonged US retreat also raises a deeper structural question: can a shared digital governance framework survive without a central coordinating actor?

As Perry World House has warned , countries may increasingly be pushed to align with competing technology stacks, infrastructure standards, and governance models. Over time, this risks producing parallel digital ecosystems with limited interoperability, different regulatory assumptions, and fewer shared safeguards.

Once embedded in infrastructure and standards, these divisions become difficult to reverse. Over time, policy divergence may become embedded in technical standards, making coordination increasingly difficult.

For partners such as India, the long-term consequence is a more complex alignment environment. Instead of operating within a broadly shared multilateral framework, countries must navigate overlapping bilateral, regional, and bloc-driven arrangements, each carrying different trade-offs around data governance, platform regulation, and state control.

The challenge is no longer just where to participate, but how to balance flexibility with influence in a system where the rules are increasingly written elsewhere.

Altogether, the memorandum should not be read as a technical clean-up of memberships or an attempt to exit ineffective bodies. It represents a step back from the processes through which rules and norms governing the internet and digital space are made. In digital governance, absence is itself a form of power: when the US stops participating in multilateral forums, it is choosing not to shape agendas or contest emerging norms. Rule-making continues in its absence, allowing standards to harden without US input and making later re-entry less effective.
