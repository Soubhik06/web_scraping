Working in the Insurance Industry, my friends and family always tell me - "That Insurance company stole my money. Insurance products suck. I would rather invest in some other product."

The following articles would be about where Insurance companies went wrong and how currently, insurance is one of the best products that any retail investor can opt for.

This is the first part about the history of life insurers, how it started, where did it go wrong, and how the entire industry has reconditioned itself today.

Will go through different products offered by Life Insurers and their value proposition in the following posts. 

Start:

The primary advice which a financial advisor gives is never mix insurance and investment. However, is this necessarily true? Life Insurance industry has changed quite a lot over the years. Most of the objections raised by these financial planners are no longer relevant. Partly due to increasingly competitive offerings and also due to policyholder centric regulations. Over the course of next articles, I will try to prove that insurance is one of the best investment options available to retail investors in today’s scenario. 

*History is who we are and why we are the way we are.*  

*– David McCullough* 

Most of the arguments cited have their roots in history. It is important to understand how insurance sector has grown in the country to understand these problems in context.  

Birth of a Behemoth  

India had its first brush with Insurance back in early 1800s. Oriental Life Insurance Company was founded in 1818. This started a gold rush later with multiple insurance companies both foreign and domestic setting up shop in India. By 1928, there were about 235 different insurance companies in India.  

Start of any industry is basically like the Wild West. With little to no regulations, lots of players come in, lots of players fail, and eventually some make it big. Government rules and regulations take time to catch up with the new age practices and are generally brought about as a result of abuse of customers or government by the dominant players. Indian Life Assurance Act, 1912 was the first act of regulation for the industry. Later more comprehensive laws were put into place when Insurance Act, 1938 came into force in order to protect interests of Insurance customers.   

By the start of 1950s, a lot of shortcomings were found in such a fragmented structure. The market for insurance was mostly concentrated in the urban areas. Premium rates charged to customers were higher than what it should ideally have been. This was mainly because of higher expenses and high profit motives of these companies. A few companies even utilized policyholder funds for their own interests rather than for their policyholders.  Between 1945 and 1955, about 25 insurance companies went bankrupt and a further 25 were sold off to others due to improper management leading to losses for policyholders.  

In 1956, life insurance business was nationalized in order to achieve objectives like: 

1. Better and economical management of life insurance business 
2. Reduction in administrative expenses 
3. Improvement in quality of service 
4. Increase volume of business to take benefits of economies of scale 
5. Maximization of social advantages that life insurance can provide through higher returns of life insurance funds consistent with safety and liquidity of invested funds.  

This was the birth of Life Insurance Corporation of India. An amalgamation of 243 different business units both domestic and foreign.  

The key point to note is that one objective missing in nationalization of Life Insurer was reduce misuse or misappropriation of policyholder funds.  

*Action speaks louder than words but not nearly as often.* 

*– Mark Twain* 

A study was carried out in 1961 by Dr. A. N. Agarwala on performance of Life Insurers prior to nationalization on the basis of their size. Basically, to find out whether nationalization of insurance companies was a good idea or not. It was found that renewal expenses for a company did go down with increase in size however it also showed that the premium rates charged were the highest for the biggest life insurers, the lapse rates did not change significantly with the size of the company, even the investment returns were found to be better for mid-sized firms rather than the large ones. In effect, there was little to no benefit from economies of scale. In essence, if data had been evaluated back in 1956, nationalization was not the solution to problems.  

This was further proved later when a study was conducted after nationalization to assess LICs performance from year 1957-75, it was found that premiums were still higher than what they should have been with respect to mortality rates, expenses as a percentage of new business premiums increased rather than going down. 

The only objective that nationalization of life insurance achieved was increase in volume of insurance business. 

*A government big enough to give you everything you want is a government big enough to take everything you have.* 

*– Gerald Ford*  

We should understand the implicit motives for nationalization of Life Insurance business. 

Insurance as a product is used to give the following benefits: 

1. Provide risk management for policyholders 
2. Accumulate savings and channel it to sectors with long term investment needs 

India as a country has always run a fiscal deficit. It is a socialist country, and a poor one at that. It has to spend money it doesn’t have so that people can survive.  Plainly speaking, government spends more than it earns through taxes. Government’s appetite to for money is huge. Although it can print money through RBI, printing money has not worked well for countries. Eventually leading to very high levels of inflation.  

The best way to finance this deficit is to borrow. And the scale is huge. For example, in FY 20-21, government has set a gross borrowing target of 1,20,00,00,00,00,000. If you have difficulties in counting the zeroes, it is 12 lakh crores.  

It is not feasible for any retail investor to loan the government the huge amount of money it needs. The most common is basically asking he banks to lend to it. The government has set SLR limits with the banks. The percentage of deposits any bank has that has to be necessarily utilized to borrow government debt. But SLR requirements keep changing with increase or decrease in deposit base.  

Government needs a long-term source of funds which would lend them consistently. Insurance comes into picture here. With premium payment terms of multiple years and policy terms extending to 100s of years it is the perfect instrument for a regular source of long-term borrowing.  

But how does this money go to the government coffers?  

Remember the issue highlighted above, *A few companies utilized policyholder funds for their own interests rather than for their policyholders.* 

Earlier SOME private companies used to collect money from policyholders and invest it in companies they had a vested interest in.  

India is unique in the sense that there is competition between the government and private sector to secure funding. India was a socialist country after independence and the government sometimes viewed private sector with mistrust.  

By nationalization, government would own the sole insurer in India. And owners decide where the policyholder funds would be invested. By being both the regulator and the owner, government could direct policyholder funds to finance its five-year plans. Nationalization of LIC was a way to direct public savings into avenues of social spending as decided by the government. This was decided by the investment policy of LIC. By 1974, 75% of the investible funds in LIC had to be necessarily invested in government securities.  

Over the years, assets managed by LIC have grown to more than 32 lakh crores.  Other countries have sovereign funds to manage national savings. India has LIC. LIC has been the investor of last resorts whenever government has to divest investments in PSUs or wants to save some mismanaged banks. It is used by the government as its own piggybank whenever it falls short on funds. 

How efficiently are policyholder funds being utilized is up to each individual’s opinion. 

The key point is nationalization of LIC bought about with a steady supply of cheap long-term funds for the government.  

However, with all thing's government, innovation and cost efficiencies go for a toss. LIC over the years has bought little to no innovation in terms of product it offers to its customers. There was little to no change in its product offerings from year 1956 when it was formed to year 2000 when privatization of insurance industry happened. 

Participatory products have been LICs forte. Participatory products are basically products with profit sharing between the company and the policyholder. 10% of net profits generated by investments are taken by the company while the other 90% is given to the customer.  

In very simplistic terms, LIC lends Rs. 100 to the government by buying government securities. LIC is guaranteed a return of Rs. 10 on it. This profit is shared between the policyholder and LIC in the ratio of 90:10. So LIC earns Rs. 1 out of the transaction. Which it again pays back to the government as dividend. The net payout from the government comes to Rs. 10 (Interest payment) - Rs. 1(Dividend payout by LIC) = Rs. 9.  Effectively, the cost of borrowing for the government becomes 9% instead of 10%. 

Since investments for these products has to be primarily in government securities, the returns were more or less equivalent or a few basis points lower than the yields in government securities. This is one of the issues cited by financial planners - Investment returns are quite low for insurance products. But more on this later.  

However, to be fair LIC of 1980s is quite different from LIC of today. Expense ratio has come down from 35% to less than 14% today. Comparable to private insurance companies. The risk weighted returns it offers to customers are sometimes better than most other investment options available to retail customers. Its network of offices and its claim settlement ratios are the envy of the private insurance companies.  

With the wave of liberalization, in year 2000, government citing low penetration of insurance, to promote competition, increase customer satisfaction and to ensure lower premiums for customers, insurance sector was liberalized. Being a heavily under insured market with a lot of potential for growth, private sector took this opportunity with open hands.  Most of the banks had realized that in order to increase their profits, they couldn’t rely on basic banking services. Apart from the interest income, the other way to increase profits was to give value added services. Insurance, Credit Cards, Brokerage Services, Cash Management Services etc.  Banks already enjoyed the trust of its customers. Why not sell other financial products in order to get commission from its sales. Most of the banks collaborated with foreign insurers to open up their own insurance arms. For example, ICICI Bank with Prudential formed ICICI Prudential Life Insurance Co. Ltd., HDFC with Standard Life formed HDFC Standard Life Insurance Co. Ltd., and SBI with BNP Paribas formed SBI Life etc. There were very few standalone insurers without the backing of banks.  

IRDAI was formed to regulate these new companies. As is at the start of any new sector, regulations take time to catch up with business. With little or no regulations, all the private companies started making hay while the sun shone.  

New categories of products, for example unit linked products, guaranteed savings products were introduced.  

None of the insurers were listed publicly, most were owned by a consortium of banks and foreign players. In most of the cases, the distributors for insurance products were the owners of the company. Insurance being a push product, distributors had to be remunerated to ensure higher market share. This remuneration had to be paid by someone, and that someone ended up being the customer 

For unit linked products, high commissions were paid out to distributors by charging equally high charges to the customers. In some cases, as high as 90% of the first-year premium. But with market in a bull run, people chose to ignore these costs. 

Then came 2008, markets all around crashed. People saw the fund values depleted to less than 50% of their peak. And the focus came back to high charges being charged by insurers. Insurance sector came under a hard look of the regulators and new regulations were notified in 2009, 2013 and 2019 and these have made the policies offered by insurers a lot more customer friendly. For example, the charges are capped and if these caps are breached, insurers have to compensate policyholders with the difference. Customers get 15 days to review the policy documents and product features and decide whether they want to stay with the policy or cancel it. Commissions are capped for all kinds of products. Customers have options to file grievances with the company or with the regulator in case of any issues that they face.  

Insurance companies are now listed and now have to answer to their customers and retail stockholders as well. This reflects in the products that they have in market as well. Most of the issues with insurance as savings instrument that financial planning experts cite are things of the past.
