 bring home a conviction under Section 411 IPC, the prosecution
 must establish that the property in possession of the accused is
 "stolen property" within the meaning of Section 410 IPC, that the
 accused received or retained such property, and that at the time of
 receiving or retaining it the accused knew or had reason to believe
 that it was stolen. 13. The first and foundational requirement is proof that the
 property is "stolen property", which presupposes proof of the
 underlying offence of theft or other predicate offence
 contemplated by Section 410 IPC. In the absence of proof of theft
 and of the complainant's proprietary interest in the very handset
 recovered, the subsequent elements of receipt or retention of
 stolen property and knowledge cannot arise. 14. In the present case, the complainant did not enter the
 witness box. There is, therefore, no substantive ocular evidence of
 theft. The e-FIR Ex. AD-1 and the statement recorded under Section 161 CrPC, which formed the basis of investigation, are not
 substantive evidence of theft. They may set the criminal law in
 motion and may be used to corroborate or contradict the maker
 when he is examined, but in the absence of his testimony they
 cannot by themselves prove the theft. 15. As regards ownership and identity of the handset, the
 prosecution relies on the invoice Ex. AD-6. While the genuineness
 of Ex. AD-6 has been admitted under Section 294 CrPC, it only
 dispenses with the formal proof of the document. It does not
 automatically translate into proof of the disputed facts unless those
 facts are otherwise borne out on the record and are reliable. The
 complainant's non-appearance has deprived the Court of any
 opportunity to test whether the invoice indeed relates to the very
 handset recovered and whether any unique identifiers such as
 IMEI numbers stand correlated. 16. The invoice Ex. AD-6 bears the IMEI number
 corresponding to the handset in question and it matches the IMEI
 reflected in the charge as well as in the CDR and CAF trail. In
 view of the admission under Section 294 CrPC, the document
 stands formally proved and a documentary link is established
 between the recovered handset and the invoice. However, such
 admission dispenses only with formal proof of the document. In
 the absence of the complainant or any seller's witness, it does not
 by itself prove the complainant's possessory title on the date of the
 alleged theft or the factum of theft. 17. Read together, Ex. AD-3 (colly) and Ex. AD-6 therefore
 demonstrate identity of the device and its usage with SIMs
 attributed to the accused during the relevant period. They do not,
 however, establish the predicate offence that would confer upon
 the handset the legal character of "stolen property" under Section

 the foundational requirement for Section 411 IPC remains
 unproved. 18. The doctrine of recent possession embodied in Illustration (a) to Section 114 of the Indian Evidence Act permits a court to
 draw a presumption that a person found in possession of stolen
 property soon after the theft is either the thief or has received
 stolen property knowing it to be stolen. The condition precedent to
 drawing such a presumption is proof of the theft and the identity
 of the stolen property. Since theft and ownership have not been
 proved here, the presumption cannot be invoked. 19. On the aspect of recovery and seizure, PW-1 deposed that
 he called the number found active in the handset and persuaded
 the user to come to the police booth, whereupon the handset was
 seized. The recovery thus took place in a public area. PW-1
 candidly admitted that though public persons were present, no
 public witness was associated with the recovery. No photographer
 or videography was arranged. While non-association of public
 witnesses is not invariably fatal, in a case where the substratum
 that the handset is stolen is itself not proved, the absence of
 independent corroboration at the stage of recovery further
 weakens the prosecution version. 20. The accused was not alleged to be the author of the theft.

 witness to link the invoice to the IMEI of the seized device. 22. It is settled that suspicion, however strong, cannot substitute
 the strict proof required in criminal trials. The burden on the
 prosecution never shifts and the standard remains proof beyond
 reasonable doubt. Where the very status of the property as stolen
 is not proved, the charge under Section 411 IPC collapses at the
 threshold. 23. On an overall appreciation of the evidence led, the Court is
 of the considered view that the prosecution has failed to establish
 that the handset allegedly recovered from the accused was stolen
 property, that it belonged to the complainant, or that the accused
 knew or had reason to believe it to be stolen. The ingredients of Section 411 IPC are not made out. 24. Resultantly, the accused Afsar Ali is acquitted of the charge
under Section 411 IPC. Judicial Magistrate First Class-05/Central
