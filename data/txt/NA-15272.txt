The government has introduced draft guidelines for e-commerce platforms, requiring self-regulatory measures to protect consumers from fraudulent practices.

The Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), under the Food and Consumer Affairs Ministry, prepared the guidelines titled “E-commerce Principles and Guidelines for Self Governance.” It has invited stakeholder comments by February 15.

The guidelines specifically prohibit any preferential treatment towards sellers or service providers. The framework also mandates the implementation of policies to prevent the sale of counterfeit products, while requiring sellers to provide accurate product descriptions and content. E-commerce entities must also operate impartially, ensuring a level playing field for all stakeholders.

“Having a consumer centric policy is of no use if it is not made known to the consumer by disclosing it properly,” it states.

The framework outlines a three-phase approach for e-commerce operations, addressing the pre-transaction, contract formation, and post-transaction stages, while also focusing on data protection practices.

E-commerce platforms must adhere to all relevant laws and regulations governing payment processing, including data protection, privacy, anti-money laundering, and other financial regulations.

a) Any personal data collected from consumers shall be used exclusively to facilitate transactions on the platform and for other purposes disclosed to the consumer at the pre-transaction stage, for which explicit consent has been obtained.

b) As custodians of consumer data, e-commerce entities must implement safeguards to prevent misuse of this data for any unauthorized commercial or alternative purposes.

This means customers should be able to access the information they need quickly and effortlessly, without having to search through various pages or sections of the website.

The earlier draft guidelines (2019) for e-commerce platforms clearly outlined the liabilities placed on e-commerce entities.

It had stated that “An e-commerce entity shall be held guilty of contributory or secondary liability if it makes an assurance vouching for the authenticity of the goods sold on its marketplace, or if it guarantees that goods are authentic.”

The current guidelines seem to dilute these provisions. The omission of explicit liability could leave consumers vulnerable, particularly in cases of defective products or unethical practices.

PRS’ analysis of the 2020 Draft Amendments to the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules highlighted the need for a clear regulatory framework to define liability among marketplaces, logistics providers, buyers, sellers, and manufacturers.

However, Nikhil Pahwa, founder of MediaNama has previously argued that liability should rest with sellers, not marketplaces, as platforms act as intermediaries facilitating transactions.

He highlighted that marketplaces cannot fully control seller actions or product quality, making 100% accountability unfeasible. While platforms like Uber or Amazon build trust through safety measures and fulfillment programs, they rely on sellers to uphold standards. Imposing liability on marketplaces could stifle innovation and competition, as these platforms aggregate resources and increase accessibility for consumers. Instead, Pahwa had suggested strengthening processes and consumer protection measures while ensuring that liability is appropriately distributed across the value chain, focusing on sellers and service providers.
