MediaNama organised a discussion on the draft amendment to the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, on November 5 in Delhi. The amendment aims to legally define synthetically generated information, including deepfakes, mandate labelling, watermarking, and metadata embedding for such content, require user declarations and automated verification by Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs), and extend due diligence obligations for intermediaries that facilitate or host synthetic content. The discussion focused on the impact of the proposed regulation on creators, platforms, and AI developers , particularly in areas of safe harbor, privacy, and freedom of expression .

On October 22, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) released a draft amendment to the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, introducing a new legal framework for synthetically generated information , commonly referred to as deepfakes. The rules aim to legally define synthetic, or AI-generated information, and mandate labelling, watermarking, and metadata embedding for such content. They also require user declarations and automated verification by Significant Social Media Intermediaries (SSMIs) and extend due diligence obligations for intermediaries that facilitate or host synthetic content.

During MediaNama’s discussion on the amendment to the rules, participants unanimously agreed that the definition of ‘synthetically generated information’ under the rules was overbroad. They suggested that, given the broad definition, almost everything from light touch-ups on pictures to augmented reality (AR) filters, smart glasses like the Meta Ray-Bans and even images from a phone camera could end up getting qualified as synthetic information under the rules.  Even for content that would conventionally fall within the scope of AI-generated information, participants argued that implementing labels would negatively affect the impact of the content. It was pointed out in an advertising test where a company tried implementing logos within the ads, there was a 10% drop in click-through rates, which is anticipated if AI-generated labels are implemented as well.

AI-generated content production services mentioned that labeling requirements could disincentivise creators from using AI tools. One participant who runs an AI video production service emphasised that they may have to create an India-specific version of the service. However, they added that even the India-specific versions would not help the government achieve the goal of curbing deepfakes since users would just end up moving to offshore platforms or open-source AI models.

One participant emphasised that the government should account for the sheer scale of content being uploaded to platforms; it would become impossible to enforce the labeling requirements. Some raised concerns about implementing the labeling mandate on live content, arguing that for live AI-generated calls, it would be hard for service providers to identify the 10% of the audio duration for which the label must be present, given that the developer wouldn’t know the length of the call. For live video containing AI-generated elements (such as AI avatars), it was argued that while a watermark could be implemented, it could cause the user to be confused about which aspects are AI-generated and which are not.

While a larger number of participants were against labeling requirements, some suggested that there could be voluntary labeling of human-generated content. They argued that in contexts such as that of CCTV footage, a person would be inclined to prove that the content is legitimate. They argued that with regard to synthetic content labeling requirements, the government should exempt minor alterations and touch-ups from labeling. In the case of both human and AI-generated content, there is a need for a provenance system that distinguishes between human and AI-generated elements.

One of the biggest points of discussion was whether AI companies fall within the scope of ‘intermediaries’ under the IT Act, 2000.  Some argued that many AI companies would not qualify as intermediaries, so applying intermediary rules to them could undermine the intent and enforceability of the new regulations. Safe harbor protections under Section 79 were seen as better suited to services that provide minimal, assistive functions rather than those that actively generate content.

On AI platform liability, one view was that responsibility should rest primarily with the user creating the prompt, since their intention shapes the harmful outcome, much like users editing content with tools such as Photoshop. Others countered that AI companies should share liability where their models hallucinate or where they profit from the system’s functioning. A suggested guiding principle was that those extracting economic value and controlling the application or distribution layer should bear more responsibility, especially in open-source scenarios. Several participants proposed a harm-based regulatory approach that allocates obligations across the AI value chain instead of trying to retrofit AI companies into the existing intermediary framework. However, there was no consensus, partly because AI’s unpredictability and the shared role of users, models, and platforms make clear liability assignments difficult.

Some participants proposed targeted safeguards to reduce the misuse of generative AI. One suggestion was to maintain a registry of public figures whose likeness cannot be replicated without explicit authorisation, covering politicians, celebrities, influencers, and other high-risk individuals. Developers also described technical measures such as real-time face verification, consent workflows, and restrictions on political or celebrity impersonation. They emphasised that ultimate enforcement must also occur at the content-publishing layer

Discussing social media platforms’ responsibilities, a participant suggested that users would be responsible for declaring AI-generated content, and platforms may be asked to add their logos to the content for tracing the content’s origins once it’s exported. Some argued that in case a user does something untoward with exported content, it would negatively impact the platform whose logo is attached to the content. Participants highlighted technical challenges that could undermine metadata-based labelling. When users upload content, platforms often transcode files, a process that can strip or alter embedded metadata. This raises the risk that social media companies could be held liable for metadata loss even when it occurs as a byproduct of standard processing.

At the same time, they warned that failure to carry out “reasonable” verification of user declarations could expose platforms to loss of safe harbour protections. What counts as reasonable will likely be clarified through judicial interpretation, creating legal uncertainty in the near term. Participants therefore urged the government to first enable broad adoption and interoperability of provenance and labelling standards—such as C2PA or similar mechanisms—before imposing strict detection and embedding requirements on platforms.

Several speakers argued that the draft rules effectively push platforms into active content moderation. They argued that obligations to proactively detect synthetic content and label it blur the line between “hosting” and “editorialising”, thereby weakening the logic of safe harbour. Even adding platform logos was seen by some as an editorial act, with reputational risks if problematic content carries the platform’s mark.

There were also constitutional concerns. Participants noted that the explanatory note frames deepfake harms in terms of misinformation, reputational damage, election interference and financial fraud. While some of these issues map onto existing grounds under Article 19(2) of the Constitution, they argued that reputational harm and misinformation per se fall outside the list of permissible restrictions on free speech. They viewed the draft as part of a broader trend of expanding control over online speech beyond those constitutionally recognised limits.

Deepfake detection was considered as an alternative to labeling, with participants highlighting that some systems reportedly reached accuracy levels close to 99.7%. They stressed, however, that performance at scale depends on how robust the underlying model is and the quality and diversity of its training data. Participants clarified that the loss of metadata generally does not undermine their ability to identify deepfakes. Their systems work primarily on visual artefacts in the pixels, not on embedded metadata.
