On April 24, 2025, MediaNama held a panel discussion on AI in Fintech. The event explored key use cases for the deployment of AI in the financial services industry, the challenges that arise with such integration, and mechanisms for responsible AI governance in fintech. The discussion advanced beyond preliminary regulatory approaches such as the Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) efforts to create responsible and ethical AI, and the Securities and Exchange Board of India’s (SEBI) mandate for accountability in AI systems.

In the past year, India’s regulatory bodies like the RBI and SEBI have begun exploring appropriate mechanisms to regulate AI use in the fintech space. MediaNama conducted a panel discussion to assess the level of AI deployment in the financial services industry, the kinds of decisions AI informs, and how a responsible governance framework could take shape. The discussion began by evaluating the current usage of AI across fintech. While fraud detection emerged as a primary use case, speakers also highlighted AI’s role in personalised investment recommendations and accessibility improvements. However, AI is still used sparingly in lending decisions due to concerns about accuracy and bias.

The discussion then turned to the challenges fintech companies face in collecting and using diverse user data for fraud prevention, personalised marketing, and investment recommendations. Panellists explained how AI and machine learning are employed to detect fraud by sharing risk scores across the ecosystem, though metrics are still evolving. User data is analysed contextually — for instance, transaction timing and device connectivity — to understand consumer behaviour without relying on personal identifiers. Investment platforms combine public market data with anonymised portfolio data to offer tailored advice. The increasing availability of diverse data points may help reduce AI bias in lending decisions. Addressing privacy concerns, participants said they often use synthetic or anonymised data in generative AI models, and that broad assumptions from industry knowledge sometimes guide credit decisions even without using foreign data.

On the issue of explainability, speakers discussed its different forms and whether it’s feasible for AI models. They noted that statistical models used by lenders typically allow for explainability, whereas traditional AI models such as large language models do not. Some panellists argued that under laws like the Consumer Protection Act, customers may have a right to transparency in how these models function — especially in financial contexts. Financial institutions tend to prefer explainable models for assessing underwriting risks. Regulators currently mandate accountability and safeguards for automated decision-making. Some global approaches distinguish between transparency and explainability and focus on maintaining audit trails to ensure fairness.

Panellists acknowledged that while AI systems provide a wide range of data to lenders, the final lending decision still rests with the financial institutions, which may be constrained despite access to robust data. Concerns were raised that AI may amplify existing biases and discrimination — for example, by relying on indicators like last names, which can correlate with sensitive factors like caste. Experts agreed that bias is an unavoidable part of AI and must be addressed through trial and error, ongoing monitoring, and feedback. Some even suggested that introducing a degree of bias intentionally could help correct historical inequities, as perfectly unbiased systems may not exist. Ensuring fairness and reducing discrimination in AI-driven lending remains a complex and evolving challenge.

Next, the panel discussed the challenges of fairness, transparency, and accountability in AI-driven lending decisions, highlighting concerns about the opacity of loan approvals and rejections. They debated whether AI-based lending should have distinct regulatory obligations compared to traditional lending, especially regarding bias and discrimination risks. The conversation also covered the tension between the need for transparency and the industry’s interest in protecting proprietary credit models and preventing manipulation.

The conversation then moved to recommendations for designing proportionate regulation of AI in fintech. Speakers suggested that market forces could help mitigate bias, but individuals facing discrimination must have accessible grievance redressal mechanisms beyond legal options. They advocated for building on existing financial regulations with audit mechanisms and AI-specific governance frameworks that consider proportionality — especially to avoid overburdening smaller players. International examples illustrate how cooperation between regulators and industry can lead to practical standards for fairness and transparency. While some panellists supported self-regulation guided by baseline principles set by regulators, others called for more clarity on AI use cases and associated risks before drafting new regulations. Concerns were raised about regulators’ technical capabilities, but it was agreed that regulatory responsibility must still be upheld, regardless of those limitations.
