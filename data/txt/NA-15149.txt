The Internet Archive suffered a cyberattack recently, leading to the leak of a user authentication database containing 31 million unique records, stated founder Brewster Kale on X (formerly Twitter). These records include the email addresses, screen names, and b-crypt password hashes. B-crypt is a technique to ‘hash’ or encrypt passwords before storage. At the same time, the website also suffered a series of distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks. In a DDoS attack, an attacker tries to overwhelm and crash a website with fake traffic.

The database was then uploaded to haveibeenpwned.com, a website where users can check if their email addresses or passwords have been leaked in a data breach. Troy Hunt, founder of the website confirmed the breach on X.

According to Bleeping Computer , news of the breach spread only when the hackers also defaced the website, greeting visitors with a popup that said, “Have you ever felt like the Internet Archive runs on sticks and is constantly on the verge of suffering a catastrophic security breach? It just happened. See 31 million of you on HIBP! [Have I Been Pwned]”

According to a chronology posted by Hunt on X, he was first alerted about the breach on September 30, contacted the Archive on October 6, and soon uploaded the database onto his website. This means that there were at least nine days before users became aware of the data breach, which seems to have occurred due to a notice posted by the hackers themselves.

An X handle called @Sn_darkmeta claimed responsibility for the DDoS attacks. They stated that the attacks were in revenge for Israel’s bombing campaign in Gaza. However, it is unknown if the same group was responsible for leaking the database and defacing the website.

The Internet Archive had previously sustained intermittent DDoS attacks in May this year.

The Internet Archive had also been battling a long court battle against major publishers like Hachette, Harper Collins, Penguin, etc. It recently lost its appeal in this copyright infringement lawsuit. The judgement requires the Archive to take down around 5 lakh books from its digital lending program.
