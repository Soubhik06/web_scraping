Update, August 21, 3:00 PM: Rajya Sabha has now passed the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Bill, 2025.

Original Post:

On August 20, 2025, the government introduced the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Bill, 2025 , in the Lok Sabha. The bill seeks to establish a comprehensive legal framework for India’s rapidly expanding online gaming sector. It covers e-sports, educational games, and social gaming while simultaneously imposing strict prohibitions on online real-money games.

According to the draft, the legislation aims to promote innovation and safeguard public interest by addressing the adverse social, financial, and psychological harms associated with money-based gaming platforms.

Furthermore, the bill proposes the creation of a national authority to oversee the sector, ensure responsible use of digital technologies, and regulate the industry in a structured manner. It cites the sector’s potential to contribute to employment, technological development, and global competitiveness, while warning against unchecked practices that have led to financial exploitation, compulsive behaviour, addiction, and threats to public order and national security.

The government has defined an “online money game” as any game played on a digital device in which a user pays fees, deposits money, or stakes other items of value in the expectation of winning monetary or equivalent returns. This definition covers games of chance, skill, or a combination of both, but specifically excludes e-sports.

Together, these sections establish a total prohibition on the offering, promotion, and financial facilitation of online real money games within India.

The government has justified the ban on online money games by citing their serious social, economic, and psychological harms. In the Statement of Objects and Reasons, the bill notes that the unchecked spread of such games has “led to grave social, economic, and psychological consequences across the country”. It points to compulsive and addictive behaviour that often results in “financial ruin, mental health disorders, and increasing incidents of fraud and exploitation”.

Additionally, the bill also highlights the disproportionate impact on youth and economically vulnerable groups, worsened by cheap internet access and aggressive advertising. The bill warns that celebrity and influencer endorsements enhance the appeal of such platforms, making them harder to regulate. It raises concern about manipulative algorithms and opaque design features that “undermine fairness and transparency”, while pushing users toward compulsive play.

Beyond individual harms, the bill links online money games to unlawful activities, including money laundering, tax evasion, and even the “financing of terrorism”, thereby posing risks to national security.

The government concludes that it is “prudent and practical in the interest of the general public to completely prohibit the activity of online money gaming, rather than attempt to regulate it.”

The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Bill, 2025, prescribes tough penalties for anyone violating the ban on online money gaming in Section 9 of the bill. The law empowers authorities to prosecute operators, advertisers, and even financial intermediaries that support such platforms.

In cases where the offence is by a company, the company is liable to be prosecuted along with every person “who, at the time the offence was committed, was in charge of, and was responsible to, the company for the conduct of that part of the business of the company as well as the company”.

Furthermore, non-compliance with directions from the government or the new authority can bring fines up to Rs 10 lakh and suspension or cancellation of registration.

The All India Gaming Federation (AIGF), the E-Gaming Federation (EGF), and the Federation of India Fantasy Sports (FIFS) wrote to Home Minister Amit Shah expressing deep concern over the proposed Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Bill, 2025.

They argued that the blanket ban on real-money gaming, including skill-based formats, would “strike a death knell for this legitimate, job-creating industry” and “cause serious harm to Indian users”.

The federations highlighted that the sector currently boasts an enterprise valuation exceeding Rs 2 lakh crore, generates around Rs 31,000 crore in revenues, and contributes over Rs 20,000 crore in annual direct and indirect tax collection. They warned that prohibition could affect an industry which is about double in size in three years’ time, as it can shut down over 400 companies, cost the exchequer Rs 20,000 crore annually, and threaten 2 lakh jobs.

Additionally, the letter also cautioned that crores of Indian players may shift to illegal offshore platforms, increasing risks of fraud, exploitation, and data insecurity.

The new bill matters because it exposes the fault line between protecting citizens and sustaining an industry. The government insists that online money games have “led to grave social, economic, and psychological consequences across the country”, from addiction and financial ruin to links with money laundering and terrorism financing. Therefore, by imposing a total prohibition, it aims to shield vulnerable groups, maintain public order, and safeguard national security.

This is in line with a recent Madras HC verdict, which upheld the Tamil Nadu government’s restrictions on real money gaming platforms, calling them public health risks.

However, industry leaders counter that this sweeping approach threatens an ecosystem valued at over Rs 2 lakh crore, employing nearly 2 lakh people. They warn that banning all formats, including legitimate skill-based games, will shutter several companies, cut off Rs 20,000 crore in annual tax revenue, and drive crores of players toward offshore, unregulated platforms where fraud and exploitation risks are higher.

The clash underscores a central dilemma: regulation is essential, but prohibition may overshoot. Strong oversight of advertising, transparency in algorithms, and protections against predatory design could address real harms without dismantling a growing sector. By choosing a blanket ban, the government risks losing economic value and global competitiveness in gaming while pushing the problem underground. This makes the bill’s outcome crucial for both users and India’s digital economy.
