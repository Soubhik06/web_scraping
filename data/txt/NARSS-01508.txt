Before the heat of this case fades like countless others, before the outrage dies down and everyone moves on with their lives, forgetting the tragedy that unfolded—I have a few things to say. Because if we don’t address the real issue now, we’ll be reading about another victim soon.
A 15-year-old boy took his own life after facing relentless bullying—his so-called friends subjected him to physical and emotional torment, forcing him into humiliating acts, attacking his appearance, and coercing him into vaping. It’s easy to be enraged at the perpetrators, but perhaps the more important question is: What made them feel that their actions were fun or justified?
Bullying, ragging, and the normalization of cruelty do not arise in a vacuum. These behaviors are reflections of a larger societal structure—one that celebrates dominance, ridicules vulnerability, and subtly rewards toxic masculinity. If children grow up consuming films that glorify power dynamics where the strong humiliate the weak for entertainment, if they see toxic behavior being trivialized or rewarded in social circles, and if they are never taught empathy as a value, it is inevitable that they will replicate what they absorb.
The Society That Breeds Silence and Fear
We live in a society where saying no—to drugs, to alcohol, to peer pressure—is seen as a weakness rather than a choice. Where the inability to “take a joke” is ridiculed, rather than questioning the nature of that joke. The fear of social exclusion pushes many into actions they wouldn’t have chosen otherwise.
The poor judiciary system in India also plays a role. Ragging, bullying, and abuse continue to be prevalent because consequences are often delayed or diluted. Even in cases of extreme violence, the legal process is sluggish, and many perpetrators escape with minimal accountability. When punishment is weak, fear of repercussions diminishes.
The Shield of Reputation: How Schools Enable Bullying
One of the biggest enablers of bullying is the shield formed in the name of school pride and reputation. Many institutions, instead of addressing issues of ragging and abuse, prefer to suppress them to maintain their image. When a school values its reputation over the safety of its students, bullying thrives unchecked.
	•	Complaints are dismissed or downplayed to avoid negative publicity.
	•	Victims are silenced with threats, making them feel isolated and helpless.
	•	Perpetrators are protected if they belong to influential families or are academically or athletically gifted.
By prioritizing image over integrity, these institutions send a dangerous message: that cruelty can be overlooked if it risks tarnishing the school’s name. This culture of silence emboldens bullies and discourages victims from speaking up.
When Teachers Become Bullies
It’s not always students who are the culprits. In many cases, faculty members themselves indulge in mental abuse, humiliating and belittling students instead of guiding them. Some teachers believe that breaking a student’s confidence is a way to discipline them. Comments like “You will never succeed in life”, “You don’t deserve to be here”, or public shaming over marks, appearance, or behavior can deeply scar a child’s mental health.
When authority figures engage in bullying, students feel even more powerless. Who do they complain to when the very people responsible for their well-being are the ones causing them harm? This toxic culture of faculty-led abuse often goes unnoticed because it is masked as “strict discipline” or “tough love,” but in reality, it contributes just as much to the problem.
Films, Social Media, and the Normalization of Aggression
Cinema, social media, and pop culture have immense influence over young minds. How often do we see movies where the “cool” character is someone who mocks, bullies, or humiliates others? Where standing up for oneself is equated to physical retaliation rather than dialogue? Where success is measured by the ability to dominate? When children repeatedly see such portrayals, they subconsciously adopt similar behaviors, believing them to be normal or even admirable.
The Way Forward
	1.	Empowering Students to Speak Up – Schools must establish anonymous reporting mechanisms where students can safely report bullying without fear of retaliation. More importantly, students should be taught that staying silent only enables bullies. By speaking up, they protect not just themselves but others who might be suffering in silence.
	2.	Holding Schools Accountable – The ‘reputation-first’ mentality must be dismantled. Schools should be legally required to disclose cases of bullying and the actions taken. Parents and students must demand transparency, and institutions that cover up incidents should face severe consequences.
	3.	Parental Awareness &amp; Background Checks – Before enrolling their child in a school based on reputation, parents must dig deeper. Talk to former students, check online forums, and ask tough questions about the school’s history with bullying. Many institutions take extreme measures to protect their image, often at the cost of student safety.
	4.	Internal Marks as a Weapon of Control – In many schools, faculty members hold power over students through internal marks, creating a toxic system where students feel forced to obey unfair demands out of fear. Whether it’s tolerating bullying, participating in humiliating activities, or staying silent about wrongdoing, students often comply just to secure their grades. This form of academic blackmail must be addressed with stricter policies that prevent the misuse of grading systems for control.
	5.	Stronger Parent-Child Communication – Many students don’t report bullying because they fear that parents will either overreact or dismiss their concerns. Instead of just asking, “How was school?”, parents must encourage open discussions, making it clear that no issue is too small to talk about.
	6.	Addressing Faculty-Led Bullying – Teachers and staff must be held to the same standards as students. Schools should have a zero-tolerance policy for faculty who mentally abuse or humiliate students. There must be an independent body where students can report faculty misconduct without fear.
	7.	Practical Peer Education – Instead of generic “anti-bullying” posters, schools should introduce real-life simulations where students experience firsthand the impact of bullying. Senior students should mentor juniors, helping them navigate social pressures instead of becoming enablers of abuse.
	8.	Reforming the Education System &amp; Legal Framework – While school-level changes matter, systemic reform is crucial. Laws against bullying should have stricter enforcement, and schools must be mandated to conduct third-party audits on student safety. Judiciary delays in bullying and harassment cases must be eliminated so that perpetrators face swift consequences.

The tragedy of the 15-year-old boy is not an isolated incident—it is a symptom of a larger problem. Until we acknowledge and address the roots of such behavior, more lives will continue to be lost to a culture that thrives on silence and normalization of violence. It’s time we redefine what is truly fun and what is simply cruel.
