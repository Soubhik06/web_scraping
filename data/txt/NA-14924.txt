Speakers talked about the impact of GenAI on editing and curation jobs, particularly in the realm of dataset management. One speaker talked about how interns had to do menial tasks that, while useful for practice, did not utilize human potential to its fullest. Now, AI models like Chat-GPT have demonstrated the ability to perform such tasks more efficiently, eliminating human error and reducing training requirements. This shift is encouraging companies to leverage AI for tasks such as coding, data scraping, and other deterministic processes.

However, in doing so GenAI also raises the minimum barrier for entry-level intellectual work, said the speaker. Companies are increasingly emphasizing AI skills in their hiring processes, though there’s ambiguity about what can constitute sufficient AI training. The impact on employment is unclear, as the number of internships hasn’t noticeably changed. However, there’s a growing skill gap, evidently seen with companies like TCS saying it will train people on how to use GenAI.

Another speaker claimed that the focus on AI-generated content has resulted in a 30% productivity hike. The speaker also mentioned how some countries like the Republic of Korea are working on a robot tax to prevent the overhaul of humans in favor of generative AI. The discussion also stressed the need for clearer definitions of AI skills to prevent fraudulent claims of expertise based solely on basic prompting abilities.

Another speaker said in rural areas, AI is enabling economic activities for women working in their native languages and creating new opportunities. Collaborations with universities are emerging, matching student credits with public sector work opportunities, indicating a growing interest in AI among both students and consumers.

Another observation was that students who could not boast of AI skills in their resumes were turning towards NGOs, redirecting a sizeable number of students from sophisticated universities towards NGO work at salary ranges of 3-4 lakhs. This trend suggests that AI is reshaping the job market, creating new niches for educated workers in the non-profit sector while potentially displacing traditional roles in larger corporations.

One speaker talked about how GenAI is showing potential to positively impact employability and job prospects in India. The person gave the example of how AI tools can help facilitate the re-entry of women into the workforce after childbirth. Another example given was in the beauty industry, where workers typically earn between INR 60-150. With the use of GenAI, women could boost their productivity by better-understanding skin tones, hair types, and product suitability. Similarly, AI can enhance their ability to customize services, potentially leading to improved earnings and job performance.

One speaker talked about how GenAI can help training and skill development and bridge barriers like language gaps. They gave the example of the AI tool Whisper which helps individuals not experienced with typing or technologies like Google Translate and voice-to-text to overcome language and literacy issues.

During the discussion there were also concerns regarding how the over-reliance on AI could be problematic in developing countries like India, potentially crowding out competition or exacerbating existing inequalities.

Additionally, the pressure on women to increase productivity through AI-enabled work-from-home arrangements may reinforce patriarchal structures rather than alleviate them.

While some speakers spoke about how GenAI can ease the workload on certain women, another speaker pointed out that jobs in sectors like textile, apparel, and food (packaging) are the ones currently under the process of being automatized.
