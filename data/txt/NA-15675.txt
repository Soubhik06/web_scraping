Recently, the Delhi High Court addressed the question of whether hyperlinking can amount to the republication of defamatory content. The case arose from a defamation suit filed by OFB Tech Pvt Ltd against the news organisation, the Morning Context, under Slowform Media Pvt Ltd.

In defamation law, republication can hold an individual liable for defamatory material even if they are not the original author. The court’s ruling examines when linking to defamatory content actively reaffirms or spreads the material, potentially holding the person sharing the hyperlink legally accountable.

Hyperlinking is a fundamental mechanism for information sharing on the internet, enabling users to navigate between web pages. Unlike direct reproduction or modification of content, hyperlinking typically functions as a reference, directing users to an external source without altering or explicitly endorsing the material.

In many cases, people share hyperlinks to provide information and facilitate access rather than to actively republish or reaffirm the linked content. However, hyperlinks can also play a passive role, especially in defamation cases, where liability depends on whether linking amplifies or legitimizes the alleged defamatory material.

Previously, in Harbhajan Singh v. State of Punjab case, the court held that every republication of a libel constitutes a new libel, making each publisher liable for their act as if they were the original author of the defamatory statement.

Note: Libel refers to defamatory statements made in a permanent form, such as writing or media, while slander involves spoken or transient defamatory remarks.

It had stated:

“Every republication of a libel is a new libel, and each publisher is answerable for his act to the same extent as if the calumny originated with him. The publisher of a libel is strictly responsible, irrespective of the fact whether he is the originator of the libel or is merely repeating it…”

Moreover, in Khawar Butt v. Asif Nazir Mir & Ors case, the court held that if a defendant republishes defamatory content with the intent to reach a different or broader audience, it constitutes a fresh cause of action.

The Bombay High Court, as well, in Indian Express Newspapers (Bombay) Pvt. Ltd. v. Dr. Jagmohan Mundhara and Serum Institute of India (P) Ltd. v. Yohan Tengra cases, reiterated that every republication of a libel constitutes a new libel, making each publisher liable as if they were the original author.

The court in Prem Peyara v. Kamla Sinha case reaffirmed the above; however, it clarified that a mere reference to defamatory content does not amount to republication (about legal proceedings, such as in cross-examination or witness testimony). Consequently, the court upheld that such references do not create a fresh cause of action for defamation or extend the limitation period for filing a defamation suit.

“On one hand, putting every hyperlink under the umbrella of republication would amount to having a chilling effect on the efficiency and fluidity implicit in the nature of the internet, while on the other hand, equating every hyperlink as a mere reference and thus putting it outside the ambit of republication would give a blanket cheque in the realm of the digital world to disseminate the defamatory content.”

The High Court further added that a hyperlinker’s liability as a republisher depends on the specific context in which the hyperlink appears. Key factors include the content and manner of hyperlinking, any implicit endorsement or repetition, and whether the hyperlink has the potential to harm a person’s reputation.

Ultimately, the crucial question in such cases is: Is it merely a hyperlink or reference, or does it go beyond that?

The concept of “reputation” remains ambiguous (perhaps dangerously so)—since authorities can invoke it to justify restrictions on human rights. Some suggest that tying “reputation” to human dignity provides a more balanced perspective.

In a 2016 ruling, the Supreme Court affirmed the constitutionality of criminal defamation, stating that it does not impose a disproportionate restriction on free speech, as safeguarding reputation is both a fundamental and human right. However, the court also clarified that the right to reputation must not come at the expense of the right to dignity.

In a notable ruling on sexual harassment and criminal defamation, a Delhi court acquitted journalist Priya Ramani in the defamation case filed by former Union Minister MJ Akbar. The court underscored that safeguarding reputation must not come at the expense of dignity and upheld the defense of “truth in public interest.”

However, the line between public interest and mere public spectacle is often blurred. Just because something grabs attention doesn’t mean it necessarily serves public interest.

In this case, the High Court ruled that hyperlinking a defamatory article can amount to republication if it extends the reach of the content in a way that could harm the defendant’s reputation.

Moreover, if the hyperlink does more than merely reference the original article—such as by repeating, redefining, explaining, paraphrasing, or endorsing its content—it effectively reinforces the defamatory material, creating a fresh impression and emphasizing it to the reader.

The court also further stated that if hyperlinking reinforces the defamatory content in a way that could damage the defendant’s reputation, it goes beyond a mere reference and qualifies as republication.

Simply put, it’s not just about mentioning the article—it’s about how the link is used. If the context clearly shows that the hyperlink highlights or endorses defamatory content, then the law may hold the person sharing it responsible for republishing the defamation.

“The mode, manner and context of hyperlinking must reveal an element of independent expression, even if subtle, in addition to the mere act of hyperlinking, for it to constitute republication,” the court stated.

“However, there can be no straight jacket formula to determine whether the hyperlink is just a reference or it is a republication. The same would have to be seen bearing in mind the facts and context of each case,” it added.

If the hyperlink merely directs users to content, it serves as a reference. But if it adds meaning, context, or influence, it could become a republication. Independent expression refers to any added commentary, context, or framing that goes beyond merely sharing a hyperlink. Even a simple caption, a summary, or the way someone embeds a hyperlink within a larger discussion can make a difference.

For a hyperlink to qualify as republication, its use must demonstrate some level of independent expression beyond simply sharing the link. However, there’s no fixed rule to determine when a hyperlink crosses that line.

Previously, in Chadha v. State , then-Finance Minister Arun Jaitley filed a criminal defamation case against opposition leader Raghav Chadha over tweets alleging corruption. Chadha sought to quash the summons, arguing that online statements should be governed by the Information Technology Act, 2000, rather than traditional defamation laws.

However, the Delhi High Court upheld the applicability of criminal defamation laws to online content, ruling that retweets could amount to republication based on their context, intent, and impact. The court clarified that simply sharing a post without endorsement may not always lead to liability, but retweets that amplify or approve defamatory content could be actionable.
