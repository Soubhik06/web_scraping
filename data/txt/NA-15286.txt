Swiggy has quietly shut down its personal errand service, Genie, across major Indian cities. The company blamed “operational constraints” for the pause but hasn’t offered a timeline for when the service might return. Genie has also disappeared from the app and help section entirely.

Swiggy launched Genie in April 2020, rebranding it from Swiggy Go , according to an Inc42 report . The service let users send and receive everyday items like lunchboxes, medicines, clothes, or documents across town. Deliveries started at Rs 40 for the first 2 kilometres. At its peak, Genie ran in around 70 cities and was used mostly for one-off errands.

The food delivery giant had pulled back on Genie before. In 2022 , it paused the service in cities like Mumbai, Bengaluru, and Hyderabad to prioritise food and grocery deliveries. The company appears to have viewed Genie as too resource-intensive compared to food and grocery delivery, based on its silence regarding the service and focus on scaling Bolt and Instamart.

Users spotted the latest shutdown before the company acknowledged it. Several Reddit users flagged Genie’s absence from the app and help menu. Some shifted to alternatives like Porter and Wefast. One Reddit user claiming to work at Swiggy wrote:

“Genie has been shut down as a business by Swiggy. P.S. I work in Swiggy.”

In another comment, they added that the decision was made to improve Instamart and food delivery by freeing up drivers: “Given the limited supply of Drivers, Genie will impact Instamart speed and availability.”

Swiggy made no mention of Genie during its Q3 FY25 earnings call , indicating a lack of focus on the service. Financial data suggests the company is scaling back on non-core services like Genie. The company does not break out Genie separately in its financials, but it likely fell under the “Platform Innovations” segment, whose revenue dropped from Rs 8.41 billion in FY22 to Rs 2.14 billion in FY24, according to Swiggy’s KPI databook . The segment has consistently reported losses throughout this period.

Meanwhile, the company has doubled down on Bolt, its 15-minute food delivery product. Bolt now operates in 450 cities and contributes nearly 9% of all food orders. The company credits Bolt with lowering delivery costs and improving order density.

Dunzo’s collapse tells a similar story. The company, which focused on hyperlocal deliveries, reported Rs 1,801 crore in losses for FY23.

Backers like Reliance Retail and Google walked away after the company failed to meet service-level targets according to a report by the Financial Times .

Dunzo’s pivot to Dunzo Daily also didn’t work. It eventually returned to errand-style deliveries, but without enough capital to sustain operations. The company now faces insolvency proceedings.

As Swiggy and others step away from errands, the Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) is trying a different approach. In a post on X in May 2025, ONDC said that over 75 percent of food and beverage orders on its network use integrated logistics, with average delivery times of 35 to 40 minutes. It partners with providers like Porter, LoadShare, Shadowfax, and Swiggy-owned Supr Daily.

However, ONDC does not operate a branded delivery app. Instead, it offers backend infrastructure that allows buyer and seller apps to connect with shared logistics partners.

But key questions remain. Can this model handle more complex deliveries, such as one-time errands or personalised tasks? Or is it better suited for standardised categories like food and groceries? Without a single brand managing the customer experience, will service quality and accountability hold up?

PhonePe’s PINCODE app recently began offering 24/7 medicine delivery through local chemists in cities like Bengaluru and Mumbai. The service doesn’t use dark stores. Instead, it partners with local pharmacies and offers teleconsultations for users who don’t have a prescription. But it’s already facing pushback. The South Chemists and Distributors Association (SCDA) has raised concerns about whether prescriptions are being verified properly, the quality of remote consultations, and the risk of fake or unsafe drugs. Like Swiggy’s Genie and Dunzo, PINCODE is running into familiar problems revolving around trust, regulation, and the difficulty of managing last-mile deliveries.

Swiggy shut down Genie because errand-style deliveries are hard to run in India. These services tried to help with everyday problems, but they couldn’t keep up with unpredictable demand, rising costs, and a constant shortage of delivery staff.

Now, Swiggy is putting its efforts into things it can control better, like Bolt and Instamart. Dunzo couldn’t make the model work either, and PINCODE is already facing roadblocks. Even the newer players are struggling with the same core challenges.

ONDC is trying something different. It’s building the backend that connects apps to shared delivery partners, rather than offering a service of its own. But it’s still unclear if that setup can handle complex, one-off deliveries.

Genie’s quiet exit shows the market is shifting. Platforms are stepping away from what doesn’t scale and leaning into models that promise consistency and control.
