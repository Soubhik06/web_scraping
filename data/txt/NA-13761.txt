The Karnataka High Court (HC) has asked Google India and three of its senior executives to provide a bank guarantee for half the amount of a penalty worth more than Rs 5 crore imposed by the Enforcement Directorate (ED). The penalty stems from allegations that the tech company broke rules under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999.

The court issued its ruling on March 20, 2025, while hearing a petition filed by the ED. The Central Government agency challenged a 2019 order from the Appellate Tribunal for FEMA violations in New Delhi, that had stayed the payment of the monetary penalty by Google.

The ED said Google India failed to get prior approval from the Reserve Bank of India before availing such a “commercial loan”. It argued that borrowing these amounts violated FEMA provisions, and effectively amounted to unauthorised borrowing of foreign exchange.

Section 6(3)(d) of FEMA gives India’s apex bank the ability to prohibit, restrict or regulate borrowing and lending of foreign exchange “in whatever form or by whatever name called.” An RBI approval is needed before an Indian entity borrows or lends foreign exchange. The ED claimed Google’s actions breached this requirement.

According to the Central Government agency, borrowing without RBI clearance in this case amounted to violating FEMA’s Section 6(3)(d).

Google India said the payments were standard business transactions, and not borrowings. There were no loan agreements, interest terms, or structured repayment schedules, the company argued, which meant the transactions didn’t fall under Section 6(3)(d) of the FEMA Act, 1999.

Google also pointed out that the RBI later approved the remittances, and the delay was subsequently regularised: meaning there was no FEMA violation.

Google had filed a case in the Appellate Tribunal for FEMA Violations, which put the penalty on hold in 2019, saying the “chances of success” of Google’s appeal were “more than the failure of appeal.” It also mentioned that paying the full amount right away could cause the tech company hardship.

Google’s appeal challenged the ED’s interpretation of the transactions and the imposed penalty. The company had asked the Appellate Tribunal to strike down the penalty. It essentially argued that the transactions with Google Ireland and Google USA didn’t qualify as borrowings under Section 6(3)(d) of the FEMA Act.

The Karnataka HC disagreed with how the Tribunal handled the stay. In its March 20 order, the court noted the Tribunal failed to consider appropriate legal precedents, adding that a complete stay on the monetary penalty won’t safeguard the ED’s interest.

It directed Google India and the three executives to submit a bank guarantee for 50 percent of the penalty. That guarantee will remain in place until the appeal is resolved.

Interestingly, Google is not the only company that the ED has investigated. The agency has opened multiple investigations into startups and digital platforms in the last few years for alleged breaches of FEMA and anti-money laundering regulations.

Google’s appeal is still to be decided upon by the Appellate Tribunal. The Karnataka HC didn’t specifically weigh in on whether the company actually broke FEMA rules. It has simply made sure the ED has some financial backing in case the Tribunal rules in its favour.

The case doesn’t exist in a vacuum. Over the past few years, the ED has stepped up action against tech and fintech companies, especially around foreign payments, complex ownership setups, and compliance gaps. Older cross-border transactions that once flew under the radar are now getting a second look.

How the Tribunal rules could shape how regulators treat past FEMA cases especially when companies rely on RBI approvals for foreign exchange-based borrowing or lending.
