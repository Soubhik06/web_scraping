It shall be unlawful for an online platform to display a fraudulent or deceptive commercial advertisement if it has accepted payment for that ad and failed to take reasonable steps to prevent it. That is the core prohibition set out in the Safeguarding Consumers from Advertising Misconduct (SCAM) Act , a bipartisan bill introduced in the US Senate by Democratic Senator Ruben Gallego and Republican Senator Bernie Moreno

Announcing the legislation, Gallego said, “If a company is making money from running ads on their site, it has a responsibility to make sure those ads aren’t fraudulent,” noting platforms currently face “almost no consequences” for such scam ads. Furthermore, Moreno added, “We can’t sit by while social media companies have business models that knowingly enable scams.”

The bill’s findings state that online platforms have become a major conduit for scam advertising, including fake giveaways, romance scams, government impersonation, health scams, and ads using AI-cloned voices and stolen images. It notes that scammers primarily use social media to initiate scams, especially among younger users, and that fraud causes consumer losses running into hundreds of billions of dollars each year, according to Federal Trade Commission (FTC) data.

Notably, it further argues that some online platforms have weakened or abandoned stricter advertiser verification processes to avoid losing ad revenue. It notes that inconsistent and optional efforts by platforms to curb scams have been unsuccessful, contributing to what the bill describes as a growing consumer confidence crisis in digital financial systems.

The SCAM Act prohibits online platforms from displaying fraudulent or deceptive commercial advertisements when they accept payment for them and fail to take reasonable steps to prevent their availability.

The prohibition applies specifically to paid commercial advertisements and does not extend to unpaid user-generated content. Liability arises only where the platform both profits from the advertisement and fails to meet the preventive obligations set out in the bill.

In addition, platforms must operate an active impersonation detection and mitigation programme, deploy automated and manual systems to detect fraudulent or deceptive commercial advertisements, and provide a clear and conspicuous reporting mechanism that allows users to flag suspected scam ads.

The bill defines an “online platform” as “any public-facing website, online service, or mobile application that predominantly provides a community forum for user-generated content”. This includes services that enable the “sharing of videos, images, games, audio files, or other content and explicitly covers social media services, social networks, and virtual reality environments”.

The term “deceptive” refers to “material misrepresentations, omissions, or practices that are likely to cause financial harm to a consumer”, interpreted consistently with FTC guidance and federal court precedent under existing consumer protection law. Furthermore, the FTC is designated as the primary enforcement authority under the Act.

The Act presumes compliance when a platform submits its fraud-detection programme to the FTC, gets approval, and actively enforces and resources it. This presumption does not apply where the FTC determines, in a specific enforcement action, that the platform failed to comply with its approved programme.

The FTC must issue regulations to implement the Act within one year of enactment and must review and update those regulations annually.

The bill removes the application of Section 230(c)(1) of the Communications Act to violations of the Act, meaning platforms cannot rely on publisher immunity for paid scam advertisements.

The problem the SCAM Act targets has long plagued India’s online ecosystem. In late January 2026, an AI-generated scam advertisement impersonating President Droupadi Murmu was found on Facebook, promoting a fraudulent investment scheme. The ad remained accessible even after being flagged, with Meta claiming it did not violate community guidelines.

Recent data from India’s advertising watchdog also shows how concentrated the issue is online. In its April–September 2025 half-yearly report , the Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) found that 97% of ad violations it processed came from digital media, with social media and influencer-led promotions accounting for a large share of misleading or non-compliant ads.

Together, these developments point to a structural issue rather than isolated failures. Indian users engage daily with global platforms that monetise ads at scale, yet accountability for deceptive paid advertising remains fragmented, relying heavily on self-regulation and post-facto takedowns.

Moreover, by placing clear legal responsibility and accountability on platforms that accept payment for scam ads, the SCAM Act offers one model for tightening enforcement around paid digital advertising.
