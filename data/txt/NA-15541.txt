Indian Railways recently announced that they have scaled up surveillance for Mahakumbh 2025. As part of this, 1,186 CCTV cameras have been deployed across nine Prayagraj (formerly Allahabad) region stations for enhanced monitoring.

This comes after Centre’s announcement of digital surveillance to be deployed at the event. The government expects a footfall of approximately 400 million (40 crore) pilgrims, making it the largest gathering of people in the world.

The Centre announced in a press release that 2,700 cameras have been deployed to monitor 400 million attendees. Each camera would cover approximately 0.015 square kilometers at Mahakumbh, which is spread across 4,000 hectares, or 40 square kilometers. This means that there would be one camera for every 1.48 lakh (1,48,148) people. However, crowd density remains a major limitation, with up to 10 million people per square kilometer in the area. Even with AI-enabled cameras, managing such densely populated areas is a monumental task.

AI-powered crowd density monitoring, along with over 340 experts at key locations, is being utilized to ensure real-time monitoring of crowd movements. Additionally, a team of 56 cyber warriors is overseeing cybersecurity, with help desks set up in all police stations to monitor online threats. Over 50,000 personnel, including paramilitary forces, are stationed to maintain security.

The Hindu reported that the Uttar Pradesh government plans to use AI-enabled cameras, Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) wristbands. This is to facilitate tracking of the headcount of pilgrims. Mobile app tracking will also be employed to monitor pilgrims’ movements.

UP Cabinet Minister Suresh Khanna outlined the three technical methods being employed to track the number of attendees at the event.

He added that the government is committed to making the Mahakumbh a fully digital event, with plans to launch a dedicated website and app. A multilingual AI-powered chatbot will assist in 11 languages, while QR code-based passes will be issued for both people and vehicles.

Notably, reports indicate that all passes provided to individuals and organisations at Mahakumbh are being issued digitally.

In addition, drone-based surveillance, integration of all locations on Google Maps, and live software to monitor the progress of 530 ongoing projects have been deployed.

Moreover, the Mahakumbh Mela is reported to face unique challenges in telecom services, as no network has ever been tested for the extraordinary density levels expected during the event. Despite the potential availability of widespread 5G coverage, the constraints of physics and the limitations of the electromagnetic spectrum suggest that network congestion could hinder pilgrims from experiencing a consistently smooth data experience.

The sheer scale of 1,48,148 people per camera presents significant challenges, about the effectiveness and ethical implications of AI-driven monitoring. In such dense crowds, AI systems, including facial recognition and anomaly detection, may struggle to accurately track individuals, leading to false positives (innocent individuals flagged as threats) and false negatives (missed identification of real threats).

Additionally, tracking individual behaviors might become increasingly difficult as AI systems attempt to process millions of faces and movements. This not only compromises the accuracy of surveillance but also raises privacy concerns. With millions of faces being scanned, data security becomes critical—there is a real risk of data breaches or misuse if safeguards are weak. Surveillance ethics also come into play, as individuals may not fully consent to being monitored. The potential for surveillance overreach also exists.

The Plank reported that Lucknow’s Safe City project, funded by the Nirbhaya Fund, aimed to enhance women’s safety using AI-enabled surveillance cameras. The system, with 1200 cameras across the city, was designed to detect suspicious behavior, including harassment and stalking, by analyzing faces and physical movements. However, the promises of emotional distress detection and predictive crime prevention proved to be overstated. The AI, despite its high-tech presentation, often generated false alarms, with 90% of the 700 monthly alerts being inaccurate. Moreover, the lack of integration with emergency services hampered its effectiveness. Officers at police stations, frustrated with unreliable technology, emphasized that basic policing needs were neglected in favor of expensive, unproven AI systems. This highlights the gap between technological promises and real-world impact, as well as the challenges in addressing systemic issues like domestic violence and police inefficiency.
