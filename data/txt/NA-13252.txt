Nikhil Pahwa: So there are instances and issues where you have to take sides. For example, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, perhaps, is one where some companies took sides, some countries took sides. Russia has a Red Web, China has its own web. Are you seeing increasing fragmentation and how do you deal with those instances when you have to take sides?

Douglas Kramer: Well, we have done everything that we can, for the very reason you state, not to take sides. I mean, we’ve fought the American government up to the U.S. Supreme Court with some of their orders asking us to do certain things. We’ve made clear that we serve a global audience.

When it comes to China, it’s a very separate Internet that we think is a very limited one and doesn’t necessarily function as well. But we have found some ways to make sure that our users who want to reach people in China, through the obstacles that have been set up, can still do that through a China network that we’ve done with partners in China. But we very much work not to make those sorts of choices.

Because again, we think you get down this road where it’s inherently, you know, we often have to talk to Western interests who sort of ask why we don’t do certain things and abuse, and we turn around and say to them, we don’t just have to solve for the narrow thing you’re trying to solve domestically in your country, we’re having to do it in a way that will also be something that we could do when we get disputes between Israel and Gaza, India and Pakistan, all sorts of other parts of the world.

And I think that usually sobers people up, how this isn’t such an easy problem, because they may be looking at two political parties within their own country or something like that, and not realising how that principle plays in many different circumstances and many different subjects.

Douglas Kramer: Yeah, no, as being a lawyer, it’s amazing how much of it is just in the definitions. Once you’ve defined your terms, the rest of it becomes a little bit easier.

I do think, though, that when we get into these conversations, so many of our efforts on the Cloudflare policy team, I’ve sort of said since I first started at the company, it’s like we don’t need carve-outs. We don’t need governments to sort of do our bidding for us or give us massive amounts of money or things like that. We just need them to understand how the Internet works.

So we spend a lot of our time talking to government officials, talking to regulatory bodies, just teaching them how the Internet works and going in that direction. Because there can be so much distrust or misunderstanding across these sorts of disputes. And this is maybe one of the prime examples.

I worked in government for an extended period before coming to Cloudflare. So I don’t know that I have the ultimate answer, but I think a lot of it starts with making sure that both sides are coming from an understanding of at least a legitimate set of base uses on either side of that.

Douglas Kramer: I think there are usually a lot of different ways to, I don’t know that I can get completely in the process of what law enforcement has available to them to do it. But, I mean, this is a question that predates the Internet.
