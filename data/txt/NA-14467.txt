Nepal’s Social Media Bill represents far more than a regulatory framework; it is an attempt to exert sweeping control over online speech and civic discourse. The government mandates licences, enforces identity verification, and threatens platforms with penalties and bans, thereby holding social media companies liable for what users publish. In effect, it forces private companies to act as censors, pre-screening content and policing speech under the constant threat of punishment.

The consequences are profound. Platforms face the burden of costly compliance in a small market, while users lose the anonymity that enables whistleblowing, citizen journalism, and dissent. The legislation’s vague language, ranging from “social disharmony” to “sovereignty” hands officials unchecked power to silence critics and suppress protest. This overreach was laid bare when the government banned 26 platforms in one sweeping move last week, based on government directives and Supreme Court rulings with similar provisions to the Bill. The decision sparked protests that ended in violence and loss of life.

Although the ban was swiftly reversed, the Bill remains, promising a permanent structure for censorship and surveillance. Regulation of harmful content is necessary, but Nepal’s model crosses the line into authoritarian control. By conflating governance with control, the government is dismantling the very space where its citizens speak, organise, and hold power accountable.

On September 4, 2025, Nepal’s government imposed a ban on several major social media platforms, citing their failure to register under newly mandated regulations requiring licensing and the appointment of local representatives. It blocked around 26 popular services such as Facebook, X, YouTube, WhatsApp, and Instagram, while platforms like TikTok and Viber remained operational because they complied with the rules.

Soon afterwards, protests erupted throughout Nepal. Demonstrators voiced broader frustrations over corruption and economic hardship in addition to social media restrictions. The situation turned deadly when security forces used water cannons, tear gas, and rubber bullets. 19 people lost their lives and hundreds were injured in the protests.

Consequently, the government quickly reversed its ban following emergency Cabinet deliberations. Communications Minister Prithvi Subba Gurung confirmed that social media platforms were restored and functioning as usual on September 9, 2025. Moreover, Home Minister Ramesh Lekhak resigned , taking moral responsibility for the violence. Nepal’s Prime Minister KP Sharma Oli also resigned amid escalating protests.

Earlier in 2025, the government had tabled the Social Media Bill in Nepal’s Federal Parliament. The Bill aimed to regulate social media platforms operating in Nepal by requiring mandatory registration, local representation, content moderation mechanisms, grievance systems, and licensing procedures. Its provisions formalise the directives and Supreme Court rulings that led to the social media ban.

Nepal’s Social Media Bill requires every company operating a social media platform to obtain a licence from the Department of Information Technology. Applicants must submit company registration papers, shareholder details, security and privacy policies, and, in the case of foreign operators, the credentials of a local contact representative.

The licence is valid for two years, and platforms must apply for renewal at least 15 days before expiry. They must attach reports on complaint handling, security measures, tax clearance, and corporate social responsibility activities. If they fail to renew, the Department can cancel the licence and order the platform to be banned.

Furthermore, the Act places extensive obligations on licensed platforms. They must prevent the circulation of content that threatens national sovereignty or harmony, develop technological safeguards, implement systems to verify the accuracy of information, and establish grievance redress mechanisms. They must also run awareness campaigns and, crucially, verify users’ real identities before granting access. The Department can impose fines for non-compliance ranging from Nepalese Rupee (NPR) 25 lakh to one crore.

Additionally, the government can direct a platform to remove content. If they fail to do so, they face fines ranging from NPR five lakh to 15 lakh, and may also be required to compensate affected individuals.

The Act also regulates users directly. It prohibits false, defamatory, obscene, hateful, misleading, or deepfake content, and bans anonymous or fake accounts. Offenders who publish posts inciting hatred or disturbing “social harmony”, “national unity”, or “national sovereignty” face fines up to NPR five lakh and imprisonment of up to five years. Cases such as phishing, sextortion, or spreading harmful anonymous content can result in up to three years’ imprisonment and fines of NPR 15 lakh.

Harleen Kaur, a researcher at Digital Futures Lab (DFL), says: “The provisions in the Nepal Social Media Bill on user accountability and penalties could have a chilling effect on online speech. By criminalising broadly defined offences such as creating fake accounts or engaging in activities that disrupt social harmony, users may become overly cautious and start self-censoring even legitimate opinions to avoid legal trouble. There is also the risk of selective enforcement. Authorities could interpret these vague provisions in a way that targets dissenting voices or critics.”

In summary, these provisions effectively remove safe-harbour protections, since platforms no longer escape liability by acting solely as intermediaries. They impose pre-censorship, as platforms must proactively filter content and comply with takedown directives. Moreover, they enforce Know Your Customer (KYC), eliminating user anonymity and raising concerns about surveillance and suppression of expression without judicial safeguards.

The Bill’s vague provisions grant authorities excessive power to remove content. For instance, the Department of Information Technology holds the authority to “immediately remove any content disseminated on social media” if it is deemed to violate the Act. However, the Bill fails to define key terms such as “social disharmony” or content that “harms” national interests, giving the government significant discretion to silence dissent.

Samik Kharel, a technology policy expert based in Kathmandu, Nepal, says: “The bill gives the government all the leverage to describe the vague ‘social harmony’ and use it according to the situation and the need. The conundrum lies in who defines the loosely penned social harmony, along with other terms and phrases. The bill also complicates social media usage by criminalizing content that disturbs the sovereignty, territorial integrity, national unity, and security.”

He refers to the Electronic Transaction Act (ETA), which criminalises the publication of posts online that go against broad terms like “public morality”, “harmonious relations”, and “spread hate or jealousy”. According to Kharel, “This Social Media Bill is an extended and refined version of the controversial ETA, which creates more vague, authoritarian, and bendable legislation. The government is trying to control the internet and content.”

Nepal’s new Bill fundamentally reshapes the legal landscape for online platforms. It holds platforms fully accountable for content published by users by introducing stringent new obligations. According to Chapter 4, Clause 12, a licensed entity must “ensure that the platform does not disseminate content that harms Nepal’s sovereignty, territorial integrity, national security, national unity, independence, dignity, or national interests, or that incites social, cultural, or religious disharmony.”

Furthermore, platforms must adopt “necessary technological measures” to prevent the sharing of illegal content. The legislation also insists platforms “prevent the uploading or dissemination of content that severely harms an individual’s character, contains hate speech, incites violence, or disrupts communal harmony.” Finally, platforms must also “ensure the privacy of social media users’ personal information and intellectual property rights.”

Consequently, these obligations make social media platforms accountable for what users publish, effectively removing safe-harbour protections. Kaur points out that broad terms like “social harmony” will discourage dissent and shrink spaces for debate. “While regulation of harmful content is necessary, obligations framed so loosely risk undermining the very role social media plays as a lifeline for communication, commerce, and civic discourse in any country,” she says.

The Bill has effectively ended online anonymity for users. First and foremost, it mandates platforms to verify the real identity of social media users before granting access, as outlined in Chapter 4, Clause 12(1)(h).

It also directly targets individuals. Clause 27 explicitly prohibits users from creating or using “an anonymous or fake identity (fake ID, page, group)” or impersonating another person. It makes it a punishable offence to establish any “anonymous, disguised, temporary, or permanent group” with the intent to spread false or misleading information that “disrupts Nepal’s sovereignty, territorial integrity, or national interests.”

Kaur explains: “Whistleblowers, citizen journalists, and activists often rely on anonymity to report on sensitive issues or hold powerful actors accountable. Fear of being traced, penalised, or surveilled may push these users to stay silent, limiting investigative reporting and civic engagement. Over time, this self-censorship could shrink the public sphere, reduce access to diverse information, and weaken the democratic role of online discourse, as people prioritise safety over free expression.”

Kharel points out that Nepal is a very small market for most social media platforms. “Setting up costly offices and grievance-handling systems is simply not worth it. Another major factor is that platforms fear that local offices would expose them to political pressure, forcing them to censor content or hand over user data. They do not want this hassle. Beyond this, the Nepal government’s registration process also adds bureaucratic red tape, requiring tax clearance certificates, company board details, video KYC checks of authorised officers, and around a dozen documents,” he says.

According to him, the average Nepali user does not care about regulation as much as they care about access to social media for information and communication. “The major stakeholders in the debate are the users, who have been sidelined in this war between the government and platforms. The users only care about access to information and communication and do not really care about legislation, policies, and regulation. The government needs to realise this and act accordingly. Regulation is important, but there is a fine line between regulation and control,” he explains.
