The copyright problem surrounding AI-generated content arguably begins at the training stage itself, rather than at the point of output. Most AI models rely on industrial-scale scraping of the internet, much of which consists of unlicensed, uncompensated, and legally disputed content.

More concerning than the proliferation of AI slop is OpenAIâs broader approach, which treats online content as copyable by default and places the burden on copyright holders to opt out, rather than requiring prior consent or licensing.

In response, MediaNama questioned whether a YouTube-style Content ID system could be adapted to flag AI-generated copyright-infringing content.

Further complicating matters, many generative AI platforms offer both commercial and non-commercial licences for AI-generated outputs. Users may attempt to claim copyright over AI slop by invoking the derivative works doctrine. However, copyright law generally requires demonstrable original creative input beyond the underlying training material to claim copyrights and potentially earn revenue protected by the respective copyright laws.

Despite lacking originality, mass-produced AI slop can still generate revenue. The core concern is that creators may commercially exploit AI-generated content, which may have been based on the unlicensed copyrighted data.

These unresolved issues are particularly relevant in India, where the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade (DPIIT) is framing policy on AI and copyright following the release of its working paper on generative AI and copyright.

Industry bodies such as the National Association of Software and Service Companies (NASSCOM), representing major technology firms including Microsoft and Google, have argued for blanket copyright exemptions for AI training. In its submission to DPIIT, NASSCOM advocated exempting text and data mining (TDM) for both commercial and non-commercial AI training.

DPIIT has also proposed a royalty-based, centralised mechanism to distribute payments to original creators through a proposed body, the Copyright Royalties Collective for AI Training (CRCAT). Under this model, creators may have limited control over pricing negotiations. While the working paper calls for greater transparency in AI training datasets, it also acknowledges the difficulty of negotiating licences with millions of rights holders without unified licensing bodies, similar to IPRS, ISAMRA, or PPL India in the music sector.

Notably, not all studios want to pursue legal action against AI-generated content. For example, reports suggest that when Warner Bros. discovered YouTube channels monetising AI-made trailers based on its IP, it opted to claim a share of the revenue rather than enforce takedowns.

As a result, the IP-related copyright debate remains unresolved, and notably untouched, by the policy YouTube chose to invoke in removing these channels.
