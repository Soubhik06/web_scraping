In the background of the recent controversy between ANI and YouTubers, other news agencies have attempted to appeal to creators by offering their content on attractive terms. Most recently, public broadcaster Prasar Bharati (PB) invited creators to sign up for its wire service PB-Shabd, announcing that it would be free. It also assured them that there would be no copyright strikes.

“Worried about copyright strikes? Not anymore,” said the X post. The service offered free content that was “safe” and “authentic.” and termed it to be a “game-changer for creators.”

In another post , the broadcaster said, “You create. We back you. No copyright claims. No takedowns. No strikes.”

The official X accounts of the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting ( MIB ) and the Press Information Bureau ( PIB ) reposted these announcements.

A few days before that, the Press Trust of India (PTI), another news wire agency, had made a post appealing to YouTube creators, offering content at attractive rates to individual YouTubers.

“We are aware of the recent issues faced by you on copyright,” said the post “To support responsible content creation, we offer individual YouTube creators highly affordable access to PTI videos for your YouTube content and for use in other social media platforms.”

Prasar Bharati-Shared Audio-Visuals for Broadcast and Dissemination (PB-SHABD) is the public broadcaster’s feed service , launched in March 2024. Subscription to the service was originally free till March this year, but the organisation recently extended it for another year.

The feed service offers audio-visual news content that is logo-free and does not require one to give credit while using it.

“One good thing that is coming out of this ANI thing is that wire services are acknowledging that people like us also need content,” said Meghnad Sahasrabhojanee, a YouTube creator.

“What ANI is doing is going at it aggressively for no reason,” he said, pointing out that the agency could easily have claimed a portion of the revenue generated by the video instead of sending a copyright strike.

“It seems like a legal strategy, honestly. Which means that they are exploiting the current YouTube system and our outdated copyright law to mint money on the backs of YouTubers,” he said.

Meghnad Sahasrabhojanee raised concerns about the third prohibition, which he pointed out was open to interpretation.

“Prasar Bharati wants to appear maybe on our side, but they are government-run agencies. So that will always be a question of how much they will let you use the clips,” he said.

The creator asked if PB would take action against him if he used a clip of the Prime Minister and made critical comments.

“The interpretation of that will depend on Prasar Bharati then. I don’t know if they will copyright strike me, but they can always take action against me. I don’t know what will happen, because it hasn’t happened yet. But there is always a chance.” he added.

PTI and PB made multiple references to copyright issues faced by YouTubers. MediaNama had previously covered how ANI had sent copyright strikes to multiple YouTubers and journalists who had used clips from the agency as part of their content. The news agency has also asked for financial compensation in exchange for withdrawing the strikes.

YouTube offers copyright strikes to copyright owners as a tool to protect their content on the platform and remove infringing material. When a channel receives a copyright strike for a video, YouTube first reviews the strike. If it considers the strike to be valid, it removes the offending video.

MediaNama had spoken to a YouTube channel that had received two copyright strikes from ANI, with an attached demand of Rs. 18 lakh. They stated that this was equivalent to the revenue they generated in 1.5-2 years. Another YouTube journalist had received almost 15 copyright strikes from the organisation and had to pay a significant amount to keep his channel.

In both instances, creators had used small clips with the duration of about 10-15 seconds as part of larger channels.

In a similar vein, popular YouTube creator Mohak Mangal, who has 4.17 million subscribers, had published a video on May 25 alleging that ANI had sent two copyright strikes and asked for over Rs. 45 lakh from him. Mangal stated that he had used small clips from ANI’s content for 9-11 seconds, all as part of significantly longer videos.

In return, ANI sued the creator for defamation in the Delhi High Court, also bringing in comedian Kunal kamra and fact-checker Muhammad Zubair as defendants for reposting the video. The agency claimed that his allegations were false and defamatory and sought a permanent injunction that would require Mangal to take down the video, stop using its trademarks, and refrain from publishing any defamatory content against ANI.

The Delhi High Court criticised the YouTuber’s choice of language in the hearing, instructing Mangal’s lawyer to delete the video and suggesting that he should have put his points in a more “civilised manner.”

For context, ANI had claimed that Mangal used words like “thug,” “extortionist,” “goonda” and “kidnapper” to refer to ANI.

In his defense, Mangal’s lawyer accused ANI of bypassing legal procedures for addressing copyright infringement, instead calling YouTubers and asking for money in exchange for withdrawing the strikes. Since YouTube deletes a channel if it receives three strikes, Mangal’s lawyer claimed that this strategy put pressure on YouTubers to accept ANI’s demands.

The contentious video in question now appears as “private” and is no longer available to view on Mohak Mangal’s channel.

Interestingly, Section 52 of India’s Copyright Act contains an exception to copyright infringement for news reporting. Legal experts MediaNama spoke to suggested that the use of ANI’s content by these YouTube journalists could fall under this section.

“It may be fair dealing, only if somebody has used copyrighted work in a way to report the news. That too, only if the use is incidental and not substantial,” Rahul Ajatshatru, an IP lawyer had explained. However, “fair dealing” is not a blanket exception, being dependent on the context and the qualitative nature of the use. Lok Sabha MP Saket Gokhale, who is also a member of the Parliament’s Standing Committee on Communications and Information Technology had raised a similar point. Without naming ANI, he had written a letter to YouTube, seeking clarifications on the platform’s policy on the fair use of news content.

The MP stated that he had received messages from numerous YouTubers who had received copyright strikes from a news agency, with attached demands for financial compensation worth lakhs of rupees.

Meghnad Sahasrabhojanee suggested that news agencies could come up with a “reasonable” package that was affordable for creators. He suggested that YouTubers would be open to paying for such subscriptions, but not if wire agencies demanded the same amounts they received from established news rooms.

“There needs to be a dialogue between creators and these agencies. They also need to understand that it’s not like we want to steal the clips. I mean, if there is a way to work together on this, why not?” Sahasrabhojanee asked.
