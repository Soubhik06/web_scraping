Amazon has filed a lawsuit against the US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) for exceeding its authority in labelling the e-commerce company a “distributor” instead of a third-party logistics provider, while directing remedial measures for the hazardous products it listed.

The case dates back to a complaint filed by the CPSC against Amazon in July 2021, urging the company to “accept responsibility for recalling potentially hazardous products” sold on its website. The Commission listed 24,000 faulty carbon monoxide detectors (failing alarms), a variety of children’s sleepwear garments (violating flammable fabric safety standards), and 400,000 hair dryers (lacking safety features against shock and electrocution) sold by Amazon. These products formed part of Amazon’s “Fulfilled by Amazon” ( FBA ) programme that allows sellers to outsource order fulfilment services including packing, shipping, and customer service to the company.

Amazon removed the standard identification numbers from these products after the CPSC’s notification and alerted affected consumers to the potential hazards. They were also offered refunds via Amazon gift cards.

However, the Commission contended that Amazon’s ‘unilateral actions’ were insufficient to address the hazards posed by the products, and did not constitute the necessary corrective measures it had ordered. Besides this, the Commission ordered Amazon to block the distribution of such products and take any necessary further actions to protect public health and safety as the CPSC directs.

On January 16, 2025, the Commission released the Final Order directing Amazon to issue additional rounds of direct notices to purchasers, provide public notice on its website, issue customers a second set of refunds based on proof of destruction, and file periodic reports regarding the status of the recall. The order was later halted following Amazon’s request.

To begin, the e-commerce giant labelled the CPSC’s complaint as an “attempt to expand its jurisdiction”, seeking to hold the company accountable for recalling the products on “Commission-dictated terms”. Amazon explains that the Consumer Product Safety Act (CPSA), 1972 , allows the Commission to issue recall orders for manufacturers, distributors, or retailers and not third-party logistics providers like Amazon. The company claims that its FBA service doesn’t manufacture, own, or sell products; instead storing and shipping them on behalf of third-party sellers who retain the title for the transaction (as required by the CPSA for third-party logistics providers).

Besides this, Amazon argues that requirements such as issuing additional notifications to customers and providing public notices exceed the Commission’s statutory authority and violate the company’s First Amendment rights. Additionally, while the CPSA mandates three remediesârefund, repair, and replacementâit does not authorise the Commission to mandate “duplicative refunds” or conditional refunds dependent on product destruction – as in the case of Amazon. The company also remarked that while it refunded every customer before May 2022, had it followed the Commission’s “bureaucratic approach”, customers would have failed to receive refunds till 2025.

Speaking of recall practices, the company contended that the CPSC violated its obligations under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA), which mandates a single round of notice and authorised notice language as Amazon used in its voluntary notices.

Concluding its arguments, Amazon requested the Court to annul the Commission’s Final Order alongside its previous ones as “arbitrary and capricious”, while declaring Amazon a “third-party logistics provider” for its FBA service, and awarding the company reasonable fees and costs for bringing this action. Besides this, the company requested the Court to declare the Commissioners’ statutory removal protections “unconstitutional” while awarding any further relief it deems just.

A similar incident also occurred in India, when the e-commerce company approached the Delhi High Court challenging a penalty worth Rs 1 lakh imposed by the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA). For context, CCPA’s order against Amazon concerned the sale of 2,265 domestic pressure cookers for “not conforming to mandatory standards” as per a notification of the Quality Control Order (QCO). Similar to the CPSC order in the US, the CCPA directed Amazon to notify all consumers who purchased the cookers, recall the products, and reimburse the customers.

As explained previously, a larger issue arises from India’s case regarding whether e-commerce companies are liable for products sold on their platforms or whether they act merely as intermediaries. Arguing along the lines of accountability and liability, the 2019 draft guidelines for e-commerce platforms allocated liabilities to such entities, a provision seemingly diluted by the 2025 draft guidelines on “E-commerce Principles and Guidelines for Self Governance”. However, MediaNama Founder Nikhil Pahwa has previously argued that liability must exist with sellers as marketplaces act as intermediaries only facilitating the transaction. Pahwa also contended that 100% accountability is unfeasible due to marketplaces not being able to fully control seller actions or product quality.

Speaking of e-commerce platforms, the safe harbour principles outlined in Section 79 of the Information Technology Act, 2000, currently insulate them from liability as intermediaries. As per this section, intermediaries must not be held liable for “any third party information, data, or communication link made available or hosted by him”. While Amazon’s counsel (in the CCPA case) based his argument on exemption from liability under Section 79 of the IT Act, CCPA’s counsel opposed this claiming that Amazon must first prove that they discharged their obligations under the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020.

India’s Consumer Affairs Ministry has previously debated a “fallback liability” provision to make e-commerce liable for fraud perpetrated by sellers. Meanwhile, a 2018 ruling in the Delhi High Court also set a benchmark of inactivity in the selling process (based on certain factors) to establish whether e-commerce platforms could lose their safe harbour principles.

The concern of accountability is also echoed with respect to Amazon’s suit filed in the US, with a nonprofit research group denounced Amazon’s legal action. William Wallace, Director of Safety Advocacy for Consumer Reports, supported Amazon’s positioning as a distributor, claiming that it aims to evade responsibility for the safety of products sold by third parties on its platform. “It’s absurd to suggest that because a company hosts a marketplace online it should be exempt from sensible requirements that help get hazardous products out of people’s homes and prevent them from being sold”, Wallace added.
