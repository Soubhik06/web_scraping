Universal and portable social security is one of the major themes of the draft National Labour and Employment Policy, which proposes to create a universal account by integrating the Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO), Employees’ State Insurance Corporation (ESIC), Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY), e-Shram , and State welfare boards.

For decades, India’s social security architecture has been fragmented, leaving millions of gig, migrant, and informal workers outside the safety net. The proposed universal social security account could finally bridge this gap.

Yet, the road to universal social security is not without hurdles, and the draft national jobs policy leaves some questions unanswered. The eligibility criteria and scope of inclusion remain unclear. Will the universal social security account cover every worker, including all gig and platform workers, migrant workers, and those in non-formal jobs? Or are there any threshold criteria such as income or number of working hours? Will gig workers already enrolled on the e-Shram portal be required to re-register? In the past, lack of awareness, documentation hurdles, and limited internet access have prevented daily-wage workers from accessing government welfare schemes.

To realize its vision of a ‘One Nation Integrated Workforce’ ecosystem, the Centre aims to implement a unified Labour & Employment Stack integrating worker identities and enterprise databases. However, the draft policy lacks any mention of guardrails to protect data privacy or prevent digital identity theft and misuse of worker data, especially for systemically vulnerable gig workers. The government must also clarify whether any fallback option exists for workers without smartphones or digital literacy.

One of the guiding principles of the draft national jobs policy is universal inclusion. The government aims to ensure that no worker, formal, informal, migrant, or gig — is left behind. However, the policy leaves room for ambiguity. How will the Ministry of Labour and Employment treat workers without formal identification, migrant workers with no online records, and those in remote areas with limited internet access?

To be truly universal and effective, the policy must clearly guarantee coverage for all categories of workers, including gig and platform workers, migrant and seasonal workers, and those in the informal sector. Any ambiguity will leave millions excluded from the safety net, said Shaik Salauddin, Founder President of the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union.

Speaking to MediaNama, he added that any contribution model for the universal social security account “cannot disproportionately burden workers” who often face low wages, sometimes less than the minimum wage and financial insecurity.

Platforms such as Zomato, Swiggy, Ola, and Uber, which rely heavily on gig and platform workers for their operations, “have benefited from these workers’ labour for years without offering basic protection. The government and employers/platforms must bear the major share of contribution,” said Salauddin, who is also Co-founder and National General Secretary of the Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers.

The Union Labour Ministry’s draft policy is also facing pushback from the industry. The All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) rejected the draft, saying it violates the tripartite process. “This comes in a blatant violation of the established tripartite process. This deeply alarming and anti-democratic move has left the workers and trade unions with a sense of shock and betrayal,” AITUC General Secretary Amarjeet Kaur told The Hindu . She called on the Union Labour Ministry to withdraw the draft and initiate discussions with central trade unions before finalising it for public opinion.

“We urge the Ministry of Labour and Employment to hold structured consultations with gig and platform workers’ unions before finalising the policy,” the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union said in a statement to MediaNama.

In response to the expanding gig economy, the Indian government introduced four labour codes in 2020, including the Code on Social Security, 2020 , to extend various social protection schemes to gig and platform workers.

According to a NITI Aayog report , there were 7.7 million gig workers in India in 2020–21, with the figure projected to reach 23.5 million by 2029–30, with an increasing number of women joining the labour force.

Despite this growth, India’s gig economy continues to grapple with algorithmic control and power imbalances, with platforms dictating how much a worker earns, which orders they receive, and even whether they keep their jobs. This lack of transparency could also affect the implementation of social security coverage for unorganised and gig workers.

Mandatory contributions without clear employer liability may prompt platforms like Flipkart, Amazon, Zomato, Swiggy, and Ola to shift costs onto workers by lowering incentives or base pay, according to the Telangana Gig and Platform Workers Union. “The policy must legally define employer obligations, preventing platforms from evading responsibility. Strong penalties must be imposed to deter such practices,” Salauddin said.

In 2023, the Indian government introduced the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA) to regulate how personal data is collected and used by companies, mandating clear consent and transparency in data practices. However, the DPDPA also falls short in protecting the rights of gig workers, especially women, who are often subjected to surveillance and harassment both at the workplace and at home.

Self-employed women, such as gig workers, mainly drove the increase in women’s workforce participation, according to the June 2025 Periodic Labour Force Survey conducted by the National Statistical Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. Under the draft policy, the government aims to raise women’s workforce participation to 35% by the end of the decade. In this context, the government must prioritise addressing the financial instability of gig workers, including women.

“To be meaningful, the universal social security scheme must guarantee comprehensive protection, including health insurance with cashless treatment, old-age pensions ensuring basic income security, maternity and paternity leave benefits, accident and disability insurance with full wage replacement during recovery, and life insurance for family security,” said Salauddin.

The draft Shram Shakti Niti 2025 marks an ambitious step towards redefining India’s labour governance. At its core is the proposal to create a universal social security account for every worker. For millions of unorganised, gig, and platform workers, the initiative could be transformative. If implemented effectively, a universal and portable social security account may provide the same level of protection that regular employees enjoy.

However, the challenges of implementing such a scheme uniformly on a large scale cannot be ignored.

The draft envisages a three-tier implementation structure, a national labour and employment policy implementation council, state labour missions, and district-level labour resource centres. How will accountability, coordination, and dispute resolution function across these levels? Since social security is partly a state subject, the universal account mechanism may also face legal challenges from state governments and in the courts.

Furthermore, a universal account will be meaningless if benefits do not reach workers who migrate to different states with separate welfare boards. The Indian government must ensure portability and universality in practice, not merely on paper.
