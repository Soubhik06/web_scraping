A recent report by the Digital India Foundation highlighted the staggering scale of illegal gambling and betting in India. The report recorded 1.6 billion visits across four analyzed platforms—Parimatch, Stake, 1xBet, and BatteryBet—in just three months.

Each operator also strategically operates multiple mirror websites to evade regulatory takedowns. For example, three mirror sites of Parimatch alone accounted for an extra 266 million visits, indicating that the actual size of the illegal gambling market is far larger than previously estimated.

Google Search trends from February 2021 to December 2024 also showed a sharp surge in interest for Dafabet, 1xBet, Parimatch, 4rabet, and Khelo24Bet, with significant spikes occurring during Indian Premier League (IPL) seasons in March and April of 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2024.

“Illegal operators sustain their operations through a highly sophisticated network of enablers, including digital advertising and marketing channels, payment ecosystems, and software providers,” the report stated.

The report further noted that while major digital platforms (such as YouTube’s recent tightened policy on gambling) prohibit betting and gambling-related promotions, enforcement remains highly inconsistent.

Previously, recognizing the escalating threat, the Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance, in its July 2023 report titled ‘Cyber Security and Rising Incidence of Cyber/White Collar Crimes,’ identified four key trends shaping the cybercrime landscape:

So, these four typologies have been primarily reported [sic]. As regards the mule account, it is mostly in India. But in the three other instances, the offshore entities are involved. It may be crypto. It may be the online betting sites. There is a clear set of online betting sites which are based out of tax havens.

Illegal operators offer various payment methods, including UPI transactions facilitated through mule accounts, cryptocurrencies, and international wallets like AstroPay ( a company previously restricted by the UK’s Financial Conduct Authority ). While some payment service providers have implemented technical measures to detect high-risk transactions—issuing warnings to users attempting payments—enforcement remains inconsistent.

The report highlighted that offshore illegal betting and gambling platforms funnel a significant share of illicit funds, enabling them to evade financial regulations and operate uninterrupted.

Previously, The Ken reported on the rising trend of “mule-as-a-service,” highlighting that overseas betting and gambling platforms are among the largest beneficiaries of the Rs. 2,500 crore mule channel funnel.

According to the Advertising Standards Council of India’s (ASCI) Half-Yearly Report for 2024-25, illegal betting and gambling advertisements have seen a sharp rise across digital media news formats. Offshore betting ads accounted for 29% of all flagged content. Of the 890 flagged ads, 831 were Instagram posts, primarily featuring tickets and tags on fan and community pages, which redirected users to offshore betting sites.

The report also noted that quantifying this market is challenging due to various methodological complexities, with the biggest hurdle being the acquisition of reliable data, as illegal activities often evade conventional data collection systems.

Recently, ASCI also partnered with Real Money Gaming bodies to combat offshore betting advertisements.

However, a major challenge in regulating these ads in India is jurisdictional limitations. Since many platforms operate from abroad, enforcing domestic laws becomes murky. Indian regulations may not apply to foreign entities, raising a crucial question: which jurisdiction’s laws will these companies actually adhere to?

Additionally, offshore gaming companies typically adhere to the laws of their country of registration, often opting for jurisdictions with favourable gambling regulations, such as Malta, Curaçao, Gibraltar, or the Isle of Man.

For example, 1xBet ( a Curaçaoan, famously controversial company ) has strategically used advertising, sponsorships, and celebrity associations to blend in with legitimate gaming platforms, despite being an offshore betting operator banned in multiple countries.

In India, the platform has continued to sponsor events such as a derby in Mysore (2023), allowing it to sustain visibility despite being officially banned in the country that same year.

Moreover, 1xBet maintains an active social media presence, using targeted ads and influencer promotions to attract Indian users. By associating with sports and esports, it mirrors the marketing strategies of legal fantasy sports platforms like Dream11 and MPL, making it harder for users to differentiate between regulated and illegal operators. As a result, enforcement becomes increasingly ambiguous, further complicating regulatory efforts.

The report added these platforms maintain numerous mirror sites, which operate in parallel and become more active during peak periods.

The report found that login credentials worked across multiple mirror sites during account creation. For example, users who registered an account on pari-matchin.com could log in on pari-match-in.com as well, ensuring smooth access even if one site went offline. This seamless access reinforced user trust and retention.

This also highlights that the true scale of operations and footprint in the Indian market are significantly larger than they appear on the surface.

Moreover, black market operators market their products on a near-equal footing with legal platforms, making a 2-3% leakage inevitable. Once these platforms generate user interest, they share alternative access channels—such as APK downloads and mirror site links—through Telegram, WhatsApp, and Discord, making enforcement even more challenging.

The Law Commission of India’s 2018 report ‘Legal Framework: Gambling and Sports Betting Including Cricket in India,; highlights the Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) authority under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007, to regulate payment processors.

It stated: “In the past, RBI has exercised its powers under this Act to achieve its objective by going after PayPal India, Neteller, and EntroPay. If the RBI were to similarly decide to go after gambling processors, the legal framework and authority, as it already exists, would, in fact, make it possible to curb the functioning of these gambling processors.”
