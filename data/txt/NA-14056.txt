India’s new Online Gaming Bill marks a significant step in distinguishing esports and social play from outright gambling. Yet, its silence on loot boxes, microtransactions, and account reselling leaves troubling gaps. Globally, regulators have already recognized that loot boxes mimic gambling mechanics, often luring minors into high-spend habits under the guise of entertainment. Belgium and South Korea, for instance, have acted decisively to curb or regulate them. In India, however, the Bill avoids mentioning these mechanics, even though they operate at the heart of many popular games.

At the same time, account selling and grey-market trades complicate the esports ecosystem. High-ranked accounts in Valorant or rare skins in Fortnite routinely fetch hundreds or even thousands of dollars on third-party platforms, despite publishers explicitly banning such practices. This shadow economy not only undermines fair competition but also exposes younger players to exploitative transactions and fraud.

Casual games with “pay-to-win” models blur the line between social play and monetized advantage. By leaving these practices unregulated, the Bill risks allowing grey markets to thrive. As India formalizes its gaming laws, lawmakers must confront the reality: gambling-like features and illicit resales already shape the industry.

The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Bill, 2025 , passed in the Rajya Sabha on August 21, 2025, seeks to create India’s first national framework for its burgeoning gaming industry. The draft law promises recognition for esports and online social games, while drawing a hard line against “online money games” where players stake money or items in expectation of monetary returns. It proposes an Authority to classify games, prohibits real money gaming, and imposes penalties of up to three years in jail and fines of Rs 2 crore for violations.

Yet, in its effort to separate skill-based esports and casual social play from gambling-like formats, the Bill leaves a regulatory grey zone. Many popular titles, such as EA FC’s (formerly the FIFA series) Ultimate Team and competitive online First Person Shooters (FPS) like Counter-Strike and Valorant, rely on microtransactions, loot boxes, or in-game assets that players resell on third-party markets.

While publishers generally forbid such trading in their terms of service, grey economies thrive, allowing players to cash out by selling accounts, coins, or skins. The Bill defines “other stakes” as virtual tokens or objects convertible to money, but does not explicitly address these resale practices. That silence raises questions on how India will treat grey-market monetisation that sits uncomfortably between esports and gambling.

Akshat Rathee, co-founder and Managing Director of NODWIN Gaming, believes “Grey-market practices like third-party skin sales and account trading could fall under the definition of ‘convertible stakes’ because they allow cash-out and sit outside the oversight of publishers or tournament organizers”.

Loot boxes and tradable skins remain one of the most contentious issues in global gaming, and India’s new Online Gaming Bill does little to resolve their status. Games such as Counter-Strike and Dota 2 allow players to purchase loot boxes, which contain cosmetic items of varying rarity. Some of these items become highly valuable in player communities. While Valve’s official Steam Community Market lets players sell items, the proceeds remain locked as Steam Wallet credit and cannot be withdrawn as cash. This restriction creates a veneer of compliance with publisher terms and consumer protection rules.

Outside official channels, third-party websites such as SkinBaron , BitSkins , CS.Money , and DMarket allow players to trade or sell skins for real money through PayPal, cryptocurrency, or other gateways. Rare items have sold for thousands of dollars, with the most expensive skin on SkinBaron priced around €99,999 ($116,830.)

Rathee explains that publishers also take action against this issue. “Valve, which runs games like Counter Strike, has taken stronger action over the years. In 2016, they issued cease-and-desist orders to skin gambling sites and later restricted container key trading to curb money laundering. More recently, they updated the Steam Code of Conduct to explicitly ban gambling-related activities and have started issuing bans to users linked with such practices. This shows that publishers themselves see the risks clearly and act against them”, he says.

The Online Gaming Bill prohibits “online money games” and defines “other stakes” as virtual objects convertible to money. This broad language could be read to cover third-party skin markets, since they clearly allow conversion of digital assets into cash. But the Bill does not mention loot boxes or grey-market trading explicitly, leaving uncertainty over whether authorities will act against offshore marketplaces. That silence risks creating confusion for players, publishers, and regulators navigating the thin line between esports, casual gaming, and gambling.

Account selling has emerged as a parallel economy across several major esports titles, where players monetize time, skill, and in-game purchases by reselling high-level accounts on third-party platforms. In FValorant, Immortal and Radiant accounts, which make up the top 1.04% of players in a server, command premium prices. On sites such as PlayerAuctions , GameBoost , and EpicNPC , Radiant accounts stacked with exclusive skins sell anywhere between $1,000 and $3,000, with prices rising sharply if the inventory includes rare cosmetic bundles.

Similarly, in Electronic Arts (EA) FC, accounts with strong squads and large coin balances are traded on platforms like Z2U.com and PlayerAuctions , often listed from $150 to over $500, depending on the rarity of player cards.

Additionally, other leading esports titles are not immune. In Fortnite, accounts featuring legacy “OG” skins such as the Galaxy or Renegade Raider are resold for $300 to over $1,000, despite Epic’s clear ban. PUBG/BGMI accounts with high ranks or rare crate items circulate on marketplaces like G2G.com , with prices spanning $50 to several hundred dollars.

Sellers frequently advertise League of Legends and Dota 2 accounts with elite ranks and rare cosmetics, turning digital progression into tradable assets. The higher the investment in skins, battle passes, or loot-box content, the higher the resale value, creating a lucrative but unregulated shadow market.

Major publishers forbid account selling and third-party currency trading in their official Terms of Service. EA outlines this in its Rules of Conduct , stating users will not “sell, buy, trade or otherwise transfer or offer to transfer your EA Account, including EA Virtual Currency and other Entitlements” without explicit approval.

Publishers like Riot Games similarly prohibit transactions involving accounts or virtual content, granting users only a “limited, non-transferable license” and forbidding any sale or exchange of their services. Epic Games’ Fortnite End User License Agreement (EULA) also prohibits players from “selling, renting, leasing, or otherwise transferring” any part of the software, including accounts and in-game content.

Together, these clauses form a legal framework against grey market trading—yet these shadow economies persist because regulation and enforcement of third-party trades remain inconsistent in practice.

A former gaming company CEO explained that game publishers have already made these third-party markets illegal, even though they remain a grey area. They opined that this would not fall under the scope of the bill in its current form.

Microtransactions and pay-to-win models have become central to the business strategy of online social games, but their regulatory status under India’s new Online Gaming Bill remains ambiguous. Social games, by definition in the Bill, are those that do not involve staking money in expectation of monetary gain. They may allow a subscription fee or a one-time access payment, but the Bill does not directly address recurring in-game purchases.

Yet most popular titles, ranging from Clash of Clans to Candy Crush, operate on microtransactions that allow players to buy in-game currency, boosters, or time-savers repeatedly. In many cases, these purchases tilt gameplay, creating “pay-to-win” dynamics that advantage those willing to spend.

The Bill does mention “other stakes,” defined as virtual credits, coins, or tokens purchased with real money. This rule could technically bring microtransaction economies under its scope if regulators treat such stakes as equivalent to money. However, because the Bill exempts online social games from the definition of “money games” as long as they do not yield financial returns, it is unclear whether microtransaction-heavy models qualify as compliant or prohibited.

That ambiguity leaves regulators, publishers, and players without clarity on whether pay-to-win practices in India’s casual gaming sector are permissible or vulnerable to future crackdowns.

Several countries have moved beyond vague warnings and definitions and have enacted concrete rules to curb loot boxes, skin resales, and account trading, but approaches differ sharply. Belgium’s Gaming Commission ruled in 2018 that paid loot boxes meet the legal definition of gambling and ordered publishers to remove or modify such mechanics for Belgian players.

South Korea has forced transparency by amending the Game Industry Promotion Act to require publishers to disclose loot-box drop rates and has ramped up enforcement since the rule took effect.

In the UK the government opted against an outright legal ban and instead pushed industry-led safeguards and disclosure guidelines, but watchdogs have since flagged widespread non-compliance by major publishers.

China takes a more interventionist stance : regulators restrict gambling-like mechanics, require odds disclosures, and aggressively police real-money trading and account-based fraud as part of broader controls on the games market.

These precedents show three regulatory patterns, i.e., prohibition (Belgium), mandated transparency (South Korea, China), and self-regulation with government oversight (UK)  that India’s Online Gaming Bill could draw on. Each model addresses different slices of the grey market (loot boxes, third-party resales, account selling), underscoring that legal clarity and enforcement design determine whether rules actually close cash-out channels or merely push them offshore.

The Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Bill, 2025, leaves lawmakers, publishers, and players with several unanswered questions. While it bans “online money games” and defines “other stakes” as tokens or objects convertible to money, the Bill does not spell out how this applies to grey-market practices.

Does the definition extend to third-party skin sales, account trading, or the resale of in-game currency like FUT coins? Regulators will need to clarify whether offshore platforms that facilitate these trades fall within the Bill’s jurisdiction.

Rathee believes that publishers are best placed to act on these issues. However, he acknowledges that “Grey-market activity can be a threat to competitive integrity in the same way betting can affect traditional sports”.

Rathee addresses in-game transactions of cosmetics and skins are a legitimate form of revenue for video games without a cash route for selling, which is what publishers are attempting.

“The challenge is to ensure that definitions remain precise so that legitimate activities such as sponsorships, prize pools, and cosmetic purchases are not restricted by accident. Prize money linked to performance is already included as acceptable in the Bill, and cosmetic purchases are clearly intended for in-game use without any cash-out route. These should remain safe monetisation channels”, Rathee adds, referring to the Bill.

At the same time, the Bill recognises esports and social games as legitimate segments, but it remains unclear how microtransactions and pay-to-win mechanics fit into this framework. If social games are limited to one-time or subscription fees, are recurring in-app purchases technically illegal, or are they permissible so long as they do not yield monetary returns? Furthermore, how will the new Authority decide what qualifies as a money game versus a social game when most titles now operate on hybrid models?

These ambiguities create uncertainty for investors, publishers, and tournament organisers. As India takes its first steps toward a unified gaming law, the central question is whether the Bill will shut down these grey markets in India via website takedowns or bans.
