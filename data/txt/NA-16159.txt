In 2004, Rajinder Kumar, the head of Research and Development at Ranbaxy, India's largest multinational pharmaceutical company, presented a report to the top brass. In it, he blew the lid open on how Ranbaxy was falsifying data on regulatory filings to get approvals for its drugs in over 40 countries.

The very next day, Kumar quit the company, displeased with how his report was received. Next year, his assistant Dinesh Thakur probed further into the company's misconduct, which he alleges led Ranbaxy to fabricate records that he browsed pornography on his office computer, forcing him to resign.

Soon after this, he went to the FDA in the US with Kumar's report. They used this information to ramp up regulatory measures against Ranbaxy facilities in the US. The Netherlands also banned drugs from certain manufacturing facilities of the pharma giant in India. 

As this was all happening, Japanese pharmaceutical company Daiichi Sankyo bought a majority of Ranbaxy's shares, making them the effective owners of the company. During this deal, Ranbaxy's owners did not disclose Kumar's report to Daiichi, let alone the fact that the FDA in the US had it. 

Had Daiichi known that Ranbaxy had falsified data, and that information about this was in the hands of the FDA, they would not have bought the company's shares, as they were, in the former's words, "pregnant with disaster". For withholding this information, Daiichi sued Ranbaxy in an arbitration court in Singapore, where a judgement was delivered this week.

This is by far one of the largest fines imposed on an Indian company by an international tribunal in a global case of fraud. The former owners and executives of Ranbaxy have to pay a whopping ₹2500 crore (over $520 million) to Daiichi Sankyo, and they have until August 22nd to appeal the decision.

The Indian Express broke the story of the judgement this week, and removed their articles on it following a threat of legal action from Ranbaxy's lawyers.
