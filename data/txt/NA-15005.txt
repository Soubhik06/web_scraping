Recently, the Election Commission (EC) initiated the process of linking Aadhaar with Electoral Photo Identity Card (EPIC) numbers. The poll body announced that it would soon commence technical discussions with the Unique Identification Authority of India (UIDAI) regarding the implementation of this initiative.

However, this contradiction—framing the initiative as both voluntary and mandatory—raises concerns about privacy, voter disenfranchisement, and electoral integrity.

The Election Commission of India (ECI) claims that Article 326 of the Indian Constitution, Sections 23(4), 23(5), and 23(6) of the Representation of the People Act, 1950/Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, as well as the Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling in G. Nirajan v. Election Commission of India , govern the process of linking Voter ID with Aadhaar.

“Voting is a constitutional right. The Supreme Court has emphatically held that when it comes to voting and exercising this right, it cannot be made contingent on the mandatory provision of Aadhaar,” stated Arjun D’Souza, Senior Legal Counsel at Software Freedom Law Centre ( SFLC.in ).

Notably, the Election Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, introduced Form 6B, requiring individuals to declare that they do not have an Aadhaar card to opt out of linking—effectively turning a voluntary process into an implicit requirement. This creates the illusion of choice: those who opt out must formally declare that they do not possess an Aadhaar number.

Following the amendment, Section 23(4) of the Act authorizes electoral registration officers to request Aadhaar numbers for voter identification. Section 23(5) mandates that individuals provide their Aadhaar details as per the prescribed rules. Meanwhile, Section 23(6) ensures that the inability to furnish an Aadhaar number—if justified by a “sufficient cause” as defined by regulations—shall not be grounds for exclusion from the electoral roll or deletion of existing entries.

Moreover, past reports suggest that Aadhaar linkage has led to widespread disenfranchisement.

Despite the Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling in G. Nirajan v. Election Commission of India, which affirmed that Aadhaar-Voter ID linking is voluntary, Form 6B remains unchanged.

It is important to point out that Aadhaar is not proof of citizenship, as reiterated in the recent press note as well:

“Voting rights can only be given to a citizen of India; an Aadhaar card only establishes the identity of a person.”

“Aadhaar is not proof of citizenship—the Supreme Court has held that, and the government has stated it on numerous occasions. So, how linking Aadhaar with the EPIC is going to somehow relieve the entire problem of duplicate or fraudulent entries still remains to be seen,” stated  D’Souza.

“Citizenship carries with it many benefits and responsibilities under the law, and it is not the same as merely establishing an identity. This is why the Supreme Court has also filtered out the purpose of an Aadhaar card, recognizing the propensity for certain loopholes in the system. Just because an individual may not have an Aadhaar card or may have one with incorrect details should not disenfranchise them—from voting, for example, or from sending their child to school. As a whole, pushing this as a mandatory scheme should not be feasible as per the Constitution,” he added.

“The submission of the Aadhaar number is not mandatory under Rule 26-B of the Registration of Electors (Amendment) Rules, 2022, and hence, the Election Commission is looking into issuing appropriate clarificatory changes in the forms introduced for that purpose.”

However, the Government of India refused to amend Form 6B, asserting that a clarification issued by the Election Commission of India was sufficient.

This approach overlooks a fundamental issue—by placing the burden on voters to justify their decision not to disclose their Aadhaar number, the process is no longer truly voluntary. Instead, it creates an implicit obligation, where individuals must actively defend their choice.

“Besides voter disenfranchisement, there is also the risk to privacy for these voters. Your Aadhaar contains personal details—linking you to your parents and also your physical address. All of this leaves open, in the entire chain, privacy risks as well as surveillance risks,” noted D’Souza.

Previously, the Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG) also raised concerns about the reliability of the Aadhaar database. In its 108-page audit report on the functioning of UIDAI, the CAG highlighted issues such as data mismatches, authentication errors, and shortcomings in record-keeping, casting doubt on the database’s integrity.

Moreover, since its inception, exclusion , data leaks , and biometric failures have marred the Aadhaar project.

“Earlier in 2015, the government attempted this entire exercise under the National Electoral Roll Purification and Authentication Programme (NERPAP), but the Supreme Court halted it, reaffirming that Aadhaar can only be mandatory for welfare schemes and PAN—not for the exercise of any constitutional right,” said D’Souza.

The Supreme Court’s ruling on NERPAP reinforces the principle that Aadhaar linkage cannot determine access to fundamental rights like voting. Enforcing such a requirement risks stripping legitimate voters—especially marginalized communities—of their democratic rights due to bureaucratic errors, data mismatches, or systemic exclusion. It also creates a dangerous precedent by allowing flawed digital systems, rather than citizenship, to determine access to constitutional rights, raising serious concerns about disenfranchisement and state overreach.
