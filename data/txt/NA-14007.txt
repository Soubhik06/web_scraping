Several former PhysicsWallah employees have alleged salary non-payment, inconsistent severance, and workplace mistreatment, claiming that the company failed to meet contractual obligations and terminated staff without due process.

They said they received only a month’s salary or less upon termination, despite appointment letters guaranteeing longer notice or severance periods. Furthermore, others described sudden dismissals during the academic year, even with positive performance evaluations.

MediaNama has spoken to former employees who are pursuing legal action against the company. They allege breaches of contract, non-payment of dues, and unfair termination practices.

Notably, the controversy emerges at a pivotal time for PhysicsWallah, which is set to become India’s first edtech company to go public. As it prepares for IPO listing, these allegations and ongoing legal challenges could raise concerns about its employment practices, compliance standards, and overall governance. Moreover, they could test investor confidence in a sector that has already faced scrutiny over profitability and workplace culture in the post-pandemic correction phase.

Several former PhysicsWallah employees have alleged a pattern of inconsistent severance, delayed or withheld salaries, and abrupt terminations, suggesting deeper concerns about the company’s internal practices. A former employee named Paras Agrawal, who worked as a Doubt Expert at the company, told MediaNama that the company offered unequal severance pay, with some receiving two months’ compensation while others received only one, despite holding similar roles and tenure.

Furthermore, others allege that the company extended their training periods beyond the agreed timelines without formal communication or additional pay, effectively delaying their confirmation and full salary entitlement.

Additionally, employees said the company often paid their monthly salaries late, and in some cases, did not pay them at all after termination. Teachers also claimed the company dismissed them mid-session, leaving students and staff without clarity.

In one instance, Agrawal mentions an employee who had recently received a “star performer” recognition but was terminated soon after without explanation. Other employees described these dismissals as unplanned and sudden, adding that performance reviews and communication from the management lacked transparency. They were also not given any Performance Improvement Plans (PIPs).

Furthermore, he also pointed out that offer letters to educators contained a clause saying that the employee cannot leave in the middle of an academic year, nor can the company terminate a contract at this time. However, he alleges that this was not followed in several terminations.

The allegations have since escalated into formal complaints. Paras Agrawal has filed grievances with the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) and the National Stock Exchange (NSE), alleging that the company’s Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) misrepresented its employment practices in its risk factors.

The complaint claims that the DRHP stated employees were only dismissed on performance grounds or for cost-restructuring, contrary to the experiences of those who say they were fired without due reason or warning. Furthermore, employees have also approached labour courts with their allegations.

Former employees also allege online that the company asked them to mislead students. A LinkedIn post by a former academic counsellor alleges that the company instructed employees to disclose only a Rs 5,000 registration fee to students and parents, hiding the actual fee, and directed them to secure “admissions at any cost.”

Furthermore, the post also alleges several other issues, including forced settlement without consent, salary cuts, and fake hiring to inflate numbers. Another post by the employee highlights workplace issues such as 12+ hour working days and a forced video presence during shifts. He further says that management paid timely salaries to some favoured employees, while others did not receive them. He adds that seniors who spoke out faced targeting, sidelining, or even forced exits.

Additionally, Agrawal also provided an example of malpractices in the admissions process. He shared screenshots of former students of the company who had reached out to him about the Physicswallah Institute of Innovation. The institute told students it is associated with the Indian Institute of Technology, Guwahati (IIT-G), and promised that enrolled students would gain access to the IIT’s alumni network and placement opportunities. However, Agrawal says he contacted IIT-G, which confirmed that the institute has never partnered with Physicswallah.

The DRHP details the company’s faculty attrition rate, which was 18.00% in FY23. Consequently, this rate dramatically surged to 40.40% in FY24. Furthermore, the firm managed to reduce the faculty attrition rate to 26.98% in FY25.

PhysicsWallah attributes faculty attrition to factors such as employees seeking better opportunities, personal commitments, or performance-related terminations. It also cites instances where educators left to join competitors or start their own ventures. The document further notes that some terminations occurred as part of cost-restructuring initiatives, while asserting that faculty attrition has declined between FY24 to FY25.

However, these explanations stand in contrast to the allegations made by several former employees, who claim that dismissals were often sudden and lacked due process. They allege the company terminated them mid-session, despite giving positive performance reviews, and used vague or inconsistent reasons to justify its decisions. Others report unpaid or delayed salaries and unequal severance packages, issues not acknowledged in the DRHP.

This growing divergence between official filings and employee accounts raises questions about PhysicsWallah’s transparency and governance practices. As the company prepares for its public listing, the gap between its stated reasons for attrition and the experiences of its former staff could draw closer scrutiny from regulators and potential investors.

PhysicsWallah recorded a net loss of Rs 1,131.1 crore in FY24, up from Rs 84.1 crore in FY23. However, in FY25, it narrowed that loss by 78%, bringing it down to Rs 243.3 crore.  On the revenue side, the edtech firm’s operating income surged to Rs 2,886.6 crore in FY24 from Rs 1,940.7 crore in FY23. In FY25, its total income (including other revenue) reached Rs 3,039.1 crore.

PhysicsWallah operates in 13 educational categories and derives most of its income from coaching services. In FY25, its operating revenue from coaching stood at Rs 2,498.6 crore, up 46% from Rs 1,715.1 crore in FY24.

Additionally, the total number of students enrolled with PW reached 48 lakh at the end of FY25, compared to 39.5 lakh in the previous year. Within this, JEE and NEET preparation courses accounted for 5.7 lakh and 9.3 lakh students, respectively.

PW’s online and offline revenue streams were nearly even in FY25. The online segment earned Rs 1,404.1 crore, while the offline segment generated Rs 1,351.9 crore. Furthermore, the company had 198 offline centres as of March 31, 2025, an increase of 57% from 126 centres a year earlier. These included 79 Vidyapeeth Centres for undergraduate entrance exam preparation and 47 Pathshala Centres, which use a hybrid teaching model combining in-person and live-streamed classes.

This confluence of allegations and corporate events assumes immense importance as PhysicsWallah prepares to launch India’s first EdTech initial public offering. Crucially, the complaints regarding arbitrary terminations and inconsistent payouts, alongside fluctuating attrition, directly challenge the company’s internal governance and employment standards. This could place its forthcoming IPO under intense regulatory and investor scrutiny.

This situation reflects a broader, urgent correction underway across the Indian EdTech sector, which already faces significant scepticism following the post-pandemic decline in online learning demand. This was seen in an 87% decrease in funding for the ed-tech sector since 2021.

Furthermore, the sector remains haunted by the spectacular collapse of its former leader, BYJU’s. The company’s downfall stems from a combination of aggressive sales tactics, governance failures, and regulatory scrutiny. Reports indicate that the company employed high-pressure sales strategies, targeting vulnerable individuals and pressuring them into purchasing courses they couldn’t afford.

Additionally, employees have reported exploitative work conditions, including long hours and unrealistic targets, contributing to a toxic workplace culture. These practices led to a significant erosion of customer trust and attracted regulatory attention , like the ED’s Rs 9000 crore FEMA violation notice.

Consequently, the market now needs to see proven business models focused on sustainable profitability and strong corporate integrity. Therefore, PhysicsWallah’s ability to manage faculty attrition and transparently address its employment disputes becomes the critical acid test, determining whether the new generation of edtech firms can restore investor faith or if the sector’s governance issues are endemic.
