“The existing safe harbour provisions in the opinion of this court do not give you protection to that extent that you can refuse and say that in the case of crimes we cannot come on board”, the Delhi High Court (HC) told the lawyers for social media platform X Corp, rejecting the company’s attempt to avoid integrating with the government’s Sahyog portal, as reported by Hindustan Times .

For context, the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C), under the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA), developed the Sahyog portal as a centralised digital system to streamline how law enforcement agencies (LEAs) issue notices to online intermediaries under Section 79(3)(b) of the Information Technology Act (IT Act).

Through Sahyog, authorised officers issue statutory notices directly, and intermediaries respond on the same platform by complying, seeking clarification, or recording reasons for non-compliance.

The statement by the court came while hearing a discharge plea filed by X regarding proceedings related to delays in furnishing information to the Delhi Police. These proceedings originated from a habeas corpus case involving a 19-year-old boy missing since January 2024.

X’s lawyers argued that the platform had fully cooperated with the investigation and is currently challenging a directive to join the Sahyog Portal in the Karnataka HC, where its appeal is pending. Furthermore, they insisted that the Delhi HC could not examine the portal within the limited scope of the missing person’s case.

The judges, however, said the case centred on law enforcement cooperation in criminal investigations and stressed that investigative agencies cannot contact dozens of intermediaries individually when they need information urgently.

Furthermore, the court emphasised that without a unified system, it would be “impossible” for officers from different police stations across the country to obtain critical information, and the judges noted that several countries, including the United States and the United Kingdom, have centralised portals for such data sharing.

X has repeatedly refused to integrate with the Sahyog portal, saying it lacks a clear statutory basis and creates what the company describes as a parallel content-blocking mechanism that circumvents established legal safeguards.

In a petition filed in the Karnataka HC in March 2025, the platform argued that the portal effectively allows state and central agencies, as well as local police, to issue blocking orders without adhering to the procedural protections laid down under Section 69A of the IT Act, the statutory process specifically designed for content blocking and judicial oversight. Furthermore, X characterised the Sahyog portal as a “censorship portal” and sought legal protection against being compelled to come on board.

However, in September 2025, the Karnataka HC dismissed X’s petition and upheld the government’s position that Sahyog serves to streamline communication between the “citizen and the (online) intermediary”, and the state can use it to “combat the growing menace of cybercrime”.

The court described the portal as an “instrument of public good” and rejected arguments that it bypassed Section 69A of the IT Act, effectively endorsing the government’s view that a centralised system was necessary to tackle cybercrime and unlawful content efficiently. However, X has filed an appeal against the Karnataka HC verdict.

As of September 2025, 94 intermediaries have been onboarded onto the Sahyog portal, according to a Right To Information (RTI) response received by MediaNama. This expanded list includes major communication and social media services, such as WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, Google, Telegram, Snapchat etc.

Another RTI confirmed that the government had onboarded several e-commerce platforms onto Sahyog, including the likes of Nykaa, Zomato, Blinkit, Flipkart, Amazon, Urban Company, Shiprocket, Reliance Retail, OLX India, Meesho, and IndiaMart.

Additionally, the government will use the Sahyog portal as the coordination layer for sharing hash data for the non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII) hash bank, a system designed to help platforms identify and remove intimate images posted and reshared without consent by assigning “hashes”: which are unique digital fingerprints to images. However, the development timeline and privacy measures remain unspecified for the bank.

The Sahyog dispute matters because it clarifies how the Indian state is enforcing online obligations through infrastructure rather than new legislation. Instead of amending the IT Act or issuing fresh rules, the government has built a centralised system under existing provisions. The Delhi HC’s observation as well as the Karnataka HC’s decision in the X case signals that courts accept this approach, particularly where criminal investigations are involved.

This shift has clear practical benefits. By giving LEAs a single, standardised channel to issue notices, the Sahyog portal reduces delays, avoids confusion over points of contact, and enables faster responses in time-sensitive investigations such as missing persons, online abuse or cyber fraud. From the Centre’s perspective, it replaces fragmented and inconsistent communication with a predictable system that works across platforms and jurisdictions.

However, the same centralisation also creates points of friction. Once authorities treat Sahyog as the default route for requests, intermediaries that remain outside it risk being seen as uncooperative, even where they raise legal or procedural objections. This narrows the space that platforms have traditionally used to scrutinise requests, seek clarification, or contest overbroad demands through internal review processes.
