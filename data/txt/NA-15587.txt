MediaNama’s Take: While protecting children online is important, blunt legal tools like blanket age restrictions risk oversimplifying a complex issue. Even countries that champion free speech and liberal democratic values, like the UK, France, and New Zealand, are enforcing or proposing strict age-gating laws. Australia has already passed a law, and Pakistan is heading in the same direction.

Such measures raise serious concerns around privacy, proportionality, and enforcement. Worse, they risk setting a dangerous precedent: authoritarian regimes may point to these democracies to justify sweeping bans on social media and suppress dissent. Policymakers must move beyond moral panic and engage with the nuances, recognising that safeguarding kids online requires thoughtful, rights-respecting regulation, not sweeping bans.

What’s the News : The Senate of Pakistan has introduced a bill that seeks to ban children under 16 from creating or using social media accounts.

Titled the Social Media Age Restriction Bill 2025, the proposal is aimed at protecting minors from the dangers of the internet, such as cyberbullying, exploitation, and harmful content.

Senators Sarmad Ali and Masroor Ahsan tabled the bill in the Senate on Monday. If passed, it would require platforms like Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and X (formerly Twitter) to prohibit users below the age threshold from signing up.

Companies that fail to comply could face fines ranging from Pakistani Rupees (PKR) 50,000 to PKR 50 lakh. Individuals who assist minors in creating accounts may also face jail time of up to six months.

The Pakistan Telecommunication Authority (PTA) will be the primary enforcement body. Apart from removing existing underage accounts, it will also frame clear-cut rules for platforms’ compliance.

The bill aligns Pakistan with Australia , which has a law in place to ban all under-16 users by December this year. Elsewhere, New Zealand’s Prime Minister Christopher Luxonsaid proposed banning children under 16 years old from using social media in May 2025

Australia’s social media ban for all under-16 users has attracted strong opinions, both in favour and against. Zoya Vickers, an Indian student currently studying in Class 10, wrote an opinion piece for MediaNama on the matter, describing the move as heavy-handed.

Justifying the ban, Australian PM Anthony Albanese had said in November 2024 that social media was harming the country’s children, and that his government had called time on it.

However, Vickers countered the effectiveness of such a move, writing, “The idea of banning social media may seem fruitful in a perfect world, but not when one considers the other aspects and factors that may circumvent or nullify the purpose of the ban.”

She cautioned that banning teens might drive them toward less regulated platforms. “These alternative spaces often lack moderation, exposing users to greater risks such as exploitation, cyberbullying, and exposure to harmful content,” Vickers wrote.

Vickers also highlighted the importance of social media for some children, especially those in unsafe or isolated environments. For them, social media can be a support system, a learning platform, and even a career-launching space.

“Many teens use platforms like Instagram and TikTok to showcase their talents, such as art, music, or writing, and to connect with opportunities that might otherwise be inaccessible,” she noted.

She further argued that such laws overlook a key contradiction: they ban teens from managing their own digital presence, while allowing vloggers in a family to broadcast their children’s lives, often without consent.

“Is banning teens from creating their own social media accounts truly more urgent than addressing this widespread exploitation?” she asked, referencing documented cases of abuse linked to family content creators.

Enforcing the Australian-style law raises technical and ethical questions. Many children bypass age restrictions by simply typing in false birth dates or using throwaway email accounts. Some have suggested advanced age-verification systems like AI-based facial recognition, but these systems pose their own problems. “Age estimation is often inaccurate and discriminatory, especially to people of colour,” Vickers warned.

She added that such methods may have a chilling effect on young users, stifling creativity and deterring them from seeking help or learning online.

“The Australian Government has washed its hands of the responsibility to educate, prepare and teach its children about how to navigate the internet,” Vickers wrote.

“We must understand it isn’t Adults vs. Teens, it’s Children, Parents, and anyone else who wants to make the internet a safer place vs. those who wish to exploit us,” she added.

The Pakistani bill is still under Senate review, and its final form could evolve. However, the debate it has sparked echoes the same concerns voiced globally: how to balance child safety with digital rights, privacy, and freedom of expression.

While the intent behind the bill is clear, Vickers urge policymakers to think beyond outright bans.

“We must recognise that complex issues cannot be tackled by simply shutting down every aspect, but should instead be carefully considered and addressed with responses and methods which, at the very least, try to cater to every detail and contradiction,” she concluded.
