Festive sales are the clearest stress test for Indian e-commerce, and year after year they expose the same dark patterns. Platforms hype deep discounts, push urgency, and sell premium memberships. However, what buyers actually face are cancellations, hidden fees, and discounts that barely exist.

The Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) told platforms to self-audit and prove they are not using these tactics. Yet complaints continue to pile up, from Flipkart’s iPhone “deals” to Amazon’s refund delays, and Myntra’s countdown tricks. Moreover, self-audits look more like a compliance checkbox rather than real consumer protection.

Ultimately, the evidence points to one conclusion: voluntary declarations will not fix the problem. Instead, India needs independent audits, mandatory public disclosures, and penalties when platforms deploy dark patterns during their biggest sales periods.

Flipkart’s Big Billion Days sale advertised iPhone 16 at Rs. 51,999 and the iPhone 16 Pro at Rs. 69,999. The offers triggered a rush of orders when the sale opened, but soon after buyers reported that Flipkart cancelled orders even after confirming successful payments.

Many received vague explanations such as “payment failure” despite proof of completed transactions. In other cases, Flipkart pushed back delivery dates by nearly a month and delayed refunds without giving clear timelines.

Customers also found that the discounts were not real. Apple’s official website listed the iPhone 16 at Rs. 69,900, almost identical to Flipkart’s “sale price”. Buyers further complained about hidden charges during checkout and Flipkart Black: the premium membership programme.

For context, Flipkart marketed Black as a way to get early access, exclusive discounts, and priority service. In practice, members saw cancellations , no special discounts, and in some cases even higher prices without the membership.

Amazon’s Great Indian Festival showed similar issues. Buyers said Amazon took their payments but cancelled the orders, leaving refunds pending for days. One customer described the experience as harassment under the cover of a mega sale.

"Amazon’s Great Indian Festival is turning into a scam — payments are taken, then orders get cancelled. My refund is still pending for 2 days. Customers are being harassed in the name of ‘mega sale’. @DoC_GoI @ConsumerCom @amazonIN — strict action is needed!" @amazonIN @PMOIndia

Customer accounts highlight the same problems again and again. Buyers said Flipkart cancelled their iPhone 14 and iPhone 16 orders despite successful payments. Others said the company stretched delivery timelines from late September to late October. Meanwhile, refunds either did not arrive or came in late. And many concluded that Flipkart misled them with ads for iPhones and Pixel phones that never actually dropped in price.

Amazon customers also alleged cancellations and refund delays during its Great Indian Festival. On Reddit, one Flipkart Black subscriber said they planned to take legal action after their order was cancelled and their membership failed to deliver any benefits.

MediaNama’s checks confirmed these complaints. Apple’s website listed the iPhone 16 at Rs. 69,900, which means Flipkart’s “sale” price was not a discount. Checkout pages on Flipkart added costs such as a ‘protect promise fee’ which only appeared at the last step. For context, this is an example of drip pricing.

In addition, Flipkart nudged buyers to add extra products like protection plans during checkout. This is basket sneaking, where add-ons are presented as part of the purchase flow rather than as an independent choice.

Apple lists the iPhone 16 at Rs. 69,900 on its official website. Flipkart’s Big Billion Days price was almost the same, raising questions about fake discounting.

The e-commerce giant’s Big Billion Days banners created urgency with slogans like “Big Billion Deals” and “Superstar Brands”, while locking discounts behind select bank cards. Amazon’s Great Indian Festival used similar tactics, relying on urgency language, card-linked offers, and inflated ‘% off’ displays.

Additionally, Myntra followed the same playbook with its “Tick-Tock Steals” banner, which used a countdown timer of 60 minutes to pressure shoppers into quick purchases. The deals often reset after the timer expired, showing that the urgency was artificial.

Moreover, the platform advertised minimum discounts of 50%, 75%, or even 80%. In practice, those reductions applied only to a handful of products while most items carried much smaller cuts. For context, this is an example of classic bait discounting.

Notably, the “Trends on Your Radar” feature nudged customers into curated lists under the guise of local preferences, blurring the line between recommendation and manipulation.

Myntra’s ‘Tick-Tock Steals’ banner used a 60-minute countdown timer and exaggerated discount claims of 50% to 80%, creating urgency and baiting shoppers with offers that rarely applied across the full catalogue.

The sales used nearly every dark pattern the CCPA defines . Flipkart’s iPhone 16 offers illustrate bait-and-switch or fake discounting, where the so-called deal either matched the regular MRP or vanished within hours. Furthermore, the framing of the sale as a once-in-a-year event and the marketing of early access through membership created false urgency, while hidden fees that appeared only at checkout was an example of drip pricing.

Flipkart Black acted as a subscription trap, where promised benefits never arrived. Whereas, bank and UPI-linked deals forced action by pushing buyers toward specific financial products.

Myntra’s countdown timers and exaggerated ‘% of’ claims reinforced false urgency and bait discounts. MediaNama also observed basket sneaking, with pre-ticked warranties, protection plans, and interface interference.

The CCPA issued an advisory that identified 13 dark patterns, including drip pricing, basket sneaking, and confirm shaming, among others. It also directed e-commerce platforms to conduct self-audits and submit declarations confirming that they did not use these tactics. Flipkart announced that it completed its audit across Flipkart, Myntra, Cleartrip, and Wholesale and submitted the findings to the Ministry of Consumer Affairs.

At the same time, the Department of Consumer Affairs formed a 19-member Joint Working Group to track dark patterns, flag violations, and recommend safeguards. The task force, chaired by Additional Secretary Bharat Harbanslal Khera, includes ministry officials, CCPA representatives, law professors, and consumer advocates. The government also showcased tools from its Dark Patterns Buster Hackathon, such as the Jagriti App and Jago Grahak Jago App, which allow consumers to flag deceptive design practices.

However, despite these measures, Flipkart’s Big Billion Days, Amazon’s Great Indian Festival, and Myntra’s festive promotions all displayed the very patterns the advisory sought to prevent. This contradiction questions the value of self-audits: platforms can declare compliance on paper while continuing to use dark patterns during their most important sales periods.

Festive sales are not just shopping events, they are the most important point of contact between India’s digital economy and millions of consumers: many of them first-time online buyers. When these sales rely on dark patterns, the harm goes beyond a single bad bargain, weakening trust in digital commerce itself.

Furthermore, the stakes are high because India is expanding digital retail through initiatives like the Open Network for Digital Commerce (ONDC) and government efforts to build confidence in online markets. If the largest platforms continue to use manipulative tactics during their flagship sales, they undermine both these policy goals and public trust in the ecosystem.

In addition, these practices distort competition: smaller platforms or sellers that avoid such tactics lose ground to companies that inflate discounts, exploit urgency, or hide fees. This tilts the market in favour of those who manipulate rather than those who compete fairly.

Finally, the absence of penalties creates another gap between policy and practice. The government has formed a task force and issued advisories, but without enforcement, platforms can treat compliance as a box-ticking exercise while continuing as before.

As a result, consumers remain exposed and regulators are sidelined at a time when digital marketplaces are becoming central to India’s economy.
