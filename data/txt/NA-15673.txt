The Goa government has published a draft of the Transport Aggregator Guidelines, 2025, under Section 67 of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 . Issued on May 20, the guidelines lay out a regulatory framework for app-based taxi and bike taxi operators in the state. The cab aggregator draft invites public feedback until June 19.

A cab aggregator must register under applicable Indian laws and open a branch office in Goa if its head office is located elsewhere. They need to pay a Rs. 5 lakh licence fee and submit a Rs. 10 lakh refundable security deposit. Licences stay valid for three years, and the transport department must process all applications within 60 working days. If a cab aggregator changes ownership or address, it must file a fresh application and pay applicable fees.

The state has yet to explain how it plans to evaluate whether new entrants, especially smaller players, are financially and operationally prepared to meet these requirements.

Only drivers with a valid Public Service Vehicle (PSV) badge and vehicles with Goan permits qualify. Aggregators must sign contracts with each driver and settle any legal disputes under Goan jurisdiction. As per the guidelines: ” The Aggregator shall ensure that all the payments to the Owner are settled within 72 hours from the completion of the journey.” If they fail to pay on time, they must compensate drivers with a 25% daily interest.

The state has not said how it will monitor payouts or enforce penalties if companies miss deadlines.

Aggregators must store all operational data in India for at least three years. They must also comply with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, the IT Act, 2000; and the Consumer Protection Act,2019. The guidelines add: “The Aggregator shall not prevent or disincentivise the driver to onboard with multiple Aggregators.”

Enforcing this clause could prove difficult unless authorities gain access to the cab aggregator onboarding records. The draft does not say how regulators will verify compliance.

The draft promises financial support to women who own and operate vehicles. The government will offer Rs. 1 lakh to buy electric cabs or Rs. 20,000 for electric two-wheelers and rickshaws. Drivers will receive half the amount upfront and the rest after completing 500 trips in a year. Cab aggregators must offer all drivers Rs. 10 lakh health insurance, and in the case of women, the policy must also cover parents and children. Companies whose fleet includes at least 20% women drivers qualify for a full licence renewal fee waiver.

The draft does not mention whether the state will support training or credit access to help women take advantage of these subsidies.

The guidelines require both the government and aggregators to establish dedicated complaint-handling systems. The Transport Department must form a Grievance Redressal Committee, chaired by the Director of Transport and supported by two Additional Directors. This committee will address complaints related to licensing, fare disputes, driver grievances, and compliance.

Separately, each cab aggregator must maintain an in-platform grievance redressal mechanism. They must resolve complaints within 48 hours of receipt. However, the draft does not outline how the government will audit or enforce these timelines across platforms.

Maharashtra already regulates platforms like Ola and Uber. Goa, in contrast, is setting up its regulatory framework for the first time.

Maharashtra allows cab aggregators to keep up to 20% of the fare. Goa requires them to pass on the entire fare amount. The former also caps surge pricing at 1.5 times and discounts at 25%, while Goa’s draft is silent on pricing mechanisms like surge caps or discounts, instead requiring drivers to receive the full government-notified fare.

Goa also skips cancellation penalties, which is a feature Maharashtra applies to both riders and drivers. Whether this omission creates confusion or imbalance remains to be seen.

On data rules, Goa explicitly requires localisation and a retention period, linking compliance to the DPDP Act. Maharashtra, by contrast, only mentions IT Act obligations and skips data retention or localisation requirements.

While Maharashtra’s policy doesn’t mention driver exclusivity, Goa bars cab aggregators from blocking drivers from joining rival platforms. Goa also relies on direct EV subsidies, while Maharashtra uses broader policy alignment.

The draft signals a shift in Goa’s approach to digital transport. For years, app-based services have struggled to gain entry due to informal controls and local opposition. By proposing enforceable rules around data localisation, full fare payouts, and gender-targeted subsidies, the government is attempting to formalise aggregator operations and bring consistency to how these platforms function in the state.

But rules on paper may not be enough. The draft lacks detail on enforcement, and it does not explain how the state will monitor driver payouts, ensure data compliance, or resolve disputes. Without a clear mechanism to check violations, implementation remains uncertain.

Meanwhile, complaints about Goa’s taxi ecosystem continue to surface. Earlier this year, a Reddit user described being forced to pay Rs. 3,000 for a 66-kilometre ride after union-linked drivers blocked a cheaper cab. Other users reported harassment of GoaMiles drivers.

In April, the North and South Goa Tourist Taxi Associations filed a police complaint against a local resident who described them as a “taxi mafia”. The unions called the term defamatory and threatened protests, according to a Herald Goa report.

The new guidelines offer a legal path for cab aggregators to enter Goa. Whether they succeed in creating actual competition or get blocked by existing power structures will depend on what happens after June 19.
