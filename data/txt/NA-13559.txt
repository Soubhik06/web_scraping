A video of Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman promoting a scheme promising massive investment returns went viral on Instagram. The governmentâs fact-check unit later identified it as a deepfake. This is not an isolated incident of deepfake. From celebrities to ministers , many have fallen victim to malicious deepfakes. A 2024 survey found that over 75% of respondents in India had seen deepfakes , and 38% had been targeted by deepfake scams. While deepfake technology can be used for benign purposes like satire , its use in impersonation , reputational harm , and misinformation has, understandably, drawn criticism. At an individual level, these can cause psychological and reputational harm, and collectively, they blur the line between reality and perception. These are being compounded in India because of its large online population , limited media literacy , fragile media ecosystem , and complex socio-political composition.

On October 22, 2025, the Indian government released draft amendments to the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 (âIT Rules, 2021â), requiring primarily Gen-AI and social media platforms to label âsynthetic informationâ towards ensuring an â open, safe, trusted, and accountable internet â.

The labelling requirements under the draft amendments would likely operate at three levels: first, at the point of content generation by the Gen-AI tool (which seems to be classified as an intermediary with this amendment, though it may not fit neatly within the IT Actâs definition of intermediary, as it generates rather than hosts content); second, at the user level, requiring disclosure when sharing or posting such content on social media; and third, at the platform level, where significant social media intermediaries like Instagram and YouTube must ensure that content is correctly labelled.

While the intent of the draft amendments is in the right direction, it is crucial to assess the implications of its ambiguous scope and proposed strategy, which could have negative consequences for user rights.

The draft amendments define âsynthetically generated informationâ as â information artificially or algorithmically created, generated, modified, or altered using a computer resource, in a manner that such information reasonably appears to be authentic or true â, which is a broad definition that covers everything from a picture which has been edited to remove a stranger to bad-faith deepfakes of celebrities. This is challenging because users may not view simple AI edits like object removal or colour correction as âsyntheticâ, while opinions may differ on Gen-AI edits such as facial adjustments , potentially negatively affecting their online experience. The draft amendments are ambiguous on whether users should disclose even minor edits, such as colour adjustments, or only when content is significantly altered or generated using AI.

The framing of âreasonably appears to be authentic or trueâ would also introduce complications at multiple levels. First, operationalising âreasonablenessâ tests across different media formats would likely be complex. For example, would realistic-looking Gen-AI satirical media fall under this definition?

Second, if the draft rule starts classifying (and labelling) anything that appears real as âsyntheticâ, it risks prioritising perceived authenticity over truth, blurring the line between fact and perception.

Third, the determination of âreasonablenessâ would likely rest with social media intermediaries. If they fail to comply with these rules, they risk losing safe harbour, which could turn them into gatekeepers of content rather than âmere conduitsâ, as they would likely err on the side of liability protection.

This opaque formulation may therefore give authorities and platforms discretion to decide what constitutes âauthenticâ, negatively impacting online speech, especially in areas like satire and political commentary , where the line between synthetic and creative expression is often blurred.

Moreover, the draft amendments require intermediaries that create or host synthetic content like Midjourney to label it clearly and prominently, using at least 10% of visual space or the first 10% of audio for disclaimers. However, these requirements seem to be wholly arbitrary, and their rationale is not supported by any evidence. Further clarity is needed on how labels should appear and what details they must include. Should a simple disclaimer suffice, or should it specify which parts were manipulated/edited and how? For example, if an AI-generated image of a political leader appearing intoxicated circulates online, should the label just state âAI-generated contentâ, or should it clarify that elements such as facial expressions or posture were altered using AI tools? The specificity of the label could majorly influence how users interpret the content and the extent to which they trust the process.

As Gen-AI becomes sophisticated, distinguishing synthetic media will be even more difficult for users and social media platforms. To establish the veracity of information, social media platforms would require two things : first, identifying whether content is synthetic, and second, determining if it is harmful. This added complexity, combined with the limitations of detection technologies , may push platforms to over-censor or filter legitimate content to avoid liability .

Additionally, labelling systems trained on existing data may become fallible against new AI generation techniques , producing false positives and negatives that further undermine trust in the information ecosystem. For example, an Instagram post by the Kolkata Knight Riders (a popular cricket club) celebrating their 2024 Indian Premier League victory was mistakenly flagged as AI-generated by Meta AI . This means that unlabelled fake content may appear real to users , while genuine content mislabelled as synthetic may be dismissed as false, eroding a shared sense of reality.

In addition to visible labelling, the draft amendments propose embedding unique metadata or identifiers to establish the veracity of the content. However, safety measures like anonymisation of such data and how it would be stored are not outlined in the draft rules. This may raise privacy concerns , as it could require collecting usersâ identifiable information and compromise their anonymity. Anonymity in the use of Gen-AI-created content will protect user rights and should not be equated with malicious activity. It provides safety to vulnerable groups , like survivors of domestic violence and sexual abuse, by allowing them to express their concerns anonymously.

More importantly, monitoring all users of synthetic media risks treating everyone as a potential offender, placing the burden on individuals to prove their innocence. This is even more concerning, as the newly released India AI Governance Guidelines suggest that tracing the origin of content is necessary to address the malicious use of deepfakes, but they do not include any safeguards for data and users.

The key question, therefore, is whether the proposed amendments are adequately positioned to address the challenges posed by synthetic content. The possible answer is no, as the proposed approach is not based on evidence and addresses harm only after it has occurred. It fails to establish proactive safeguards to prevent the creation of malicious synthetic media. This is crucial because harmful synthetic content can be viewed or shared thousands of times before it is detected, labelled and removed.

Even labelling such content as AI-generated after its creation does little to mitigate the harm experienced by victims. For example, once a non-consensual intimate image is made using Gen-AI, the damage persists even if it is later flagged and removed. One reason for the misalignment between stated objectives and process could be the lack of scientific evidence on what constitutes synthetically generated information, associated harms, and the absence of impact assessments on labelling.

The effect of technologies like Gen-AI, including deepfakes, has exposed existing societal fault lines, underscoring the need to strengthen trust in public institutions and invest in media literacy and education, rather than relying only on fallible top-down interventions like labelling.
