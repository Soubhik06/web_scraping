Meta chief executive officer (CEO) Mark Zuckerberg took the witness stand in a social media addiction trial in Los Angeles this month, where he was questioned on whether Instagram’s design contributed to addictive behaviour among young users and harmed their mental health.

In court, Zuckerberg faced questioning from Mark Lanier, the lawyer representing a woman identified as KGM, who began using Instagram and YouTube in childhood and alleges that the platforms intensified her depression and suicidal thoughts. Her team challenged Zuckerberg’s testimony by citing internal communications and alleged that he misled Congress in 2024, when he said the company did not set goals of maximising time spent on their platforms.

“We strongly disagree with these allegations and are confident the evidence will show our longstanding commitment to supporting young people”, a Meta spokesperson told Al Jazeera.

Before proceedings began, TikTok and Snap Inc. reached settlements with the plaintiff ahead of trial, leaving Meta and Google’s YouTube to contest the claims before a jury. That narrowed focus has consequently sharpened scrutiny on Meta’s internal deliberations, particularly around how it assessed risks to teenagers and enforced its own age rules.

Notably, as testimony has unfolded, the court has partially released several Meta internal documents into evidence. This material, spanning from 2016 to 2023, offers a detailed look at how Meta targeted teen engagement and marketed to children.

Planning documents from 2016 show that a focus on increasing teenage users was already embedded at the company level. In a 2016 internal email, executives described the “overall company goal” to be “total teen time spent”. Another email reads, “Mark has decided that the top priority for the company in H1 2017 is teens”.

By 2018, internal reviews acknowledged stagnation and decline. A Youth Team Review noted that “Teen MAP in the US and western countries continues to decline”. At the same time, it identified strategic pillars — Kids, Tweens, and Teens — and highlighted product efforts such as launching Messenger Kids, Meta’s first in the “under 13 category”.

Furthermore, Meta’s internal documents frame declining teen engagement not as incidental, but as a core strategic problem. A 2018 review states plainly: “The teen population on Facebook is decreasing and so one of the key goals of the Youth Growth team is to get teens onto Facebook and to stay there.” Furthermore, the same document outlines the underlying assumption that “teens’ lives and social network revolve around their school”, and argues that improving the “high school experience for them on Facebook will increase their engagement”.

Crucially, internal analysis connected engagement to school-based networks. A chart in the review concluded: “The more friends you have, the more likely you are to use FB (Facebook). However, the quality of your friends also matters – if they come from your own school, you are more likely to be engaged.”

Having defined the problem, the documents show Meta building products around school identity and network effects. A draft “High School Directory – Product Messaging & Launch Doc” explains that Instagram would allow users to add their high school to their profile. This will lead them to a directory of students in the same high school and allow them to easily direct message (DM) them.

The target audience is listed as “US Teens” aged “13–17”. The behavioural insight is explicit: “We know that as many as 20% of high school students in the US already add their high school name (and often grade) to their profile. This behaviour is primarily driven amongst students who are transitioning into high school from different middle schools.”

Meanwhile, a separate plan titled “Everyone on Facebook HS edition” sets the goal to “Learn whether school blasts can tip schools from inactive to active via network effects and incrementally increase teen MAP in those schools.” For context, “school blasts” refers to coordinated, school-wide outreach or activation efforts aimed at students within a specific school.

Internal emails about the “Lifestage” product, a 2016 video-based social networking app for teens that was discontinued the next year, reveal a model in which schools would initially be unlocked, and then any school with sufficient student requests could be added. The author notes that they could not predict “which schools exactly will be on the app after it launches”, but refers to schools “we’d talk about publicly”.

Taken together, these exhibits demonstrate a coordinated effort: first, identify that teens engage more with peers from their own school, then design directories, communities, and launch tactics to activate entire campuses. And finally, manage the rollout school by school (Exhibit 63). Pertinently, this strategy repeatedly relies on social density and peer visibility to drive engagement.

Beyond product design, the documents reveal structured marketing and engagement tactics aimed at teens. An “Overview: 2018 Instagram Ambassador Program” states that the programme would “recruit and onboard teen tastemakers to act as our plug at local high schools” and “drive product adoption through word of mouth for upcoming launch initiatives”. The “ideal ambassador profile” is listed as someone who is “13–17 years old” and “well-connected to peers”. The programme included NDAs (non-disclosure agreements) and parental permission, as well as DM groups to manage teen ambassadors.

Meanwhile, internal marketing summaries show segmentation by demographic cohort. A 2020 Instagram marketing document includes a section titled “The Youthful Optimist” that asks, “What pain points do Black users aged 13–14 experience on Instagram?” And then points to users reporting algorithmic bias,  experiences of cultural appropriation, and race-based negativity.

At the product level, teams also worked to remove friction. A 2018 update admits: “We discovered a longstanding issue (since at least mid-2017) where the Facebook Fake Account classifier is inadvertently checkpointing legitimate Teen accounts.” The same document discusses “Private Mode”, applying principles from “Finstas” (a common trend on Instagram where users make second private accounts with less followers) such as “smaller audiences” and “plausible deniability” from guardians.

Finally, a heavily redacted slide dec k titled “Controlling the Narrative” outlines strategic opportunities for youth growth and refers to leveraging school-based networks to drive acquisition and engagement, including positioning Instagram as central to managing peer relationships during key transition periods, such as moving or graduating from schools.

The internal documents surfaced in this trial arrive as governments around the world intensify efforts to curb children’s access to social media. A 2021 internal Meta survey submitted in court found that 19% of Instagram users aged 13–15 said they had received unwanted nude images, while 8% reported seeing self-harm content on the platform. Those findings, disclosed during the proceedings, demonstrate that the company had quantified the risks young users were encountering.

Set against that data, the internal planning material outlined above in the article assumes greater weight. Executives tracked “total teen time spent”, prioritised teen growth, built high school directories and activated “school blasts” to tip campuses into higher engagement.

Therefore, the policy debate shifts from speculation to evidence. Lawmakers in the European Union (EU), India, and across Asia are considering age-based restrictions on social media. As these internal documents and survey findings become public, this trial could shape how regulators judge whether platforms can adequately protect children, whether they are even willing to, or whether governments will move towards outright bans and structural constraints.
