The Supreme Court, in two separate hearings this week, signalled that it may soon evolve a legal framework for online free speech, especially in cases involving satire, comedy, and offensive content. As reported by Bar and Bench , the first case involves YouTubers like BeerBiceps and Samay Raina, the second concerns cartoonist Hemant Malviya’s challenge to a Madhya Pradesh High Court order denying him anticipatory bail for a controversial Facebook cartoon. In both matters, the Court raised concerns over the rise of “offensive” speech online and suggested that India may need a test to determine the boundaries of expression in the digital age.

As MediaNama founder Nikhil Pahwa argued earlier , the Ranveer Allahbadia case has become a flashpoint in India’s ongoing battle over free speech and digital content regulation. He noted how such controversies often follow a familiar pattern: outrage over offensive speech, political amplification, platform takedowns, and eventually, regulatory pressure or judicial intervention. He warned that “the hecklers clearly have the veto in India.”

That pattern appears to be unfolding again. On July 15, a bench of Justices Surya Kant and Joymalya Bagchi heard a set of petitions involving content posted by comedians and podcasters, including Ranveer Allahbadia (BeerBiceps), Samay Raina, and others. The petitioners are facing allegations of obscene and insensitive remarks made during podcast episodes. One petition, filed by the Cure SMA India Foundation, accused Raina of making derogatory comments about persons with disabilities and the cost of treatment for spinal muscular atrophy.

Justice Surya Kant remarked during the hearing: “Suppose a race takes place between Article 19 and Article 21 ; Article 21 has to trump over Article 19.” He added that the proposed guidelines to regulate content must balance these two rights. The Court also called for an “open debate” on these guidelines, inviting stakeholders to weigh in on how such content should be regulated.

While the Court has not yet passed any orders on the content guidelines, it has directed several comedians to continue appearing in person for the hearings. It warned that their conduct will be scrutinised and any future absence will be taken seriously.

Siddhant Saroha, Partner at Raven Law LLP and an advocate at the Supreme Court, told MediaNama: “In constitutional jurisprudence, Article 21 of the Constitution, which guarantees the right to life and personal liberty, is often accorded primacy over Article 19, which protects various freedoms, including the freedom of speech and expression. This is because Article 21 is widely regarded as one of the most fundamental and overarching rights in the constitutional framework.

Saroha further added, “In situations where there is a conflict between the two, the Supreme Court may curtail the scope of rights under Article 19 to the extent necessary to safeguard the rights enshrined under Article 21, thereby ensuring a harmonious construction of fundamental rights. While the Court has consistently upheld the freedoms guaranteed under Article 19, it has also emphasised that such freedoms are not absolute and come with a ‘duty’ towards the society.”

On the same day, a different bench of Justices Sudhanshu Dhulia and Aravind Kumar heard cartoonist Hemant Malviya’s plea seeking anticipatory bail. Malviya had previously been denied bail by the Madhya Pradesh High Court for posting a cartoon that depicted Prime Minister Narendra Modi and the RSS in a manner the court found inflammatory. He is facing charges under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita and Section 67A of the Information Technology (IT) Act.

The Supreme Court granted Malviya interim protection from arrest but also issued a warning, according to a report by Mint . The bench told Malviya that if he continued to post offensive content online, the state would be free to take legal action. Justice Dhulia remarked, “Log kisi ko bhi, kuch bhi keh dete hain” (People say anything to anyone), and noted that freedom of speech appeared to be getting misused. He added , “There should be a test. We’ll have to do something on this.”

Additional Solicitor General KM Nataraj presented screenshots of other allegedly objectionable posts. Vrinda Grover, appearing for Malviya, objected to their inclusion, pointing out that the screenshots were undated, fell outside the scope of the FIR, and prejudiced the case. She argued that while the cartoon may be in poor taste, it did not amount to a legal offence. Grover also said Malviya was willing to delete the post.

The Court did not permit Malviya to delete the new material and said the State could return to the Court if more objectionable content surfaces. The bench will hear the matter again in August.

These cases don’t exist in a vacuum. The Supreme Court is now directly engaging with the question of where to draw the line on online free speech. In both hearings, judges pointed to the need for a legal test to decide what kind of content deserves protection in today’s digital space. This is especially significant because such a move could take place without any legislative direction from Parliament.

Saroha explained: “Technically, the Supreme Court is empowered to evolve judicial standards or tests in the absence of specific legislation, and such action is constitutionally sound. This authority stems from Article 141 of the Constitution , which mandates that the law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India. While the Court has recognised the need for comprehensive statutory guidelines to regulate free speech, particularly in the context of online and social media content, it has also invited an ‘open debate’ and called upon the Central Government to frame appropriate regulatory measures. However, it remains to be seen in what manner the new guidelines affect the rights of an individual. The difficulty is that we don’t know if these guidelines will be open to public scrutiny or not.”

He also warned: “The new speech guidelines might have an impact on the way creators create content. It might limit their creativity in a negative way because they will have to protect themselves while creating new content for the people.”

The trend goes beyond Malviya and the Youtubers. Governments and platforms have repeatedly taken down satirical content using unclear legal processes. The government blocked Juice Media’s India video on YouTube using provisions of the Indian Penal Code. Internet service providers censored Tamil outlet Vikatan’s website after it published a cartoon of Prime Minister Modi. Instagram also removed the meme page Savala Vada after it mocked the government, without offering any legal explanation.

Saroha flagged a broader issue: “An often overlooked dimension in the contemporary discourse surrounding online speech and content regulation is the collective rights of the audience or the broader public who consume, appreciate, and actively support such content.”

He pointed out that creators like Samay Raina have massive followings, with millions of people engaging with their content voluntarily and positively. “In a constitutional democracy that values free speech under Article 19(1)(a), the right to create and express is intrinsically linked to the audience’s right to receive information and ideas.”

“When content is challenged based solely on objections from a vocal minority, there arises a constitutional tension. Whose rights take precedence? The creator’s right to express? The minority’s right to not be offended? Or the majority’s right to access and enjoy that content?”, Saroha opined.

He warned that allowing the displeasure of a few to dictate what content remains online “sets a dangerous precedent that undermines the pluralistic character of Indian constitutional democracy.”

Any legal test the Court creates, he added, must consider not only the rights of those offended but also the rights of the majority who choose to engage with that speech.
