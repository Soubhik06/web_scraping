a position where he would have been if there had been no breach
or promise would have been fulfilled and to restore the position
of the injured party where he was before the promise was made
and in the course of which the promise altered his position by
putting reliance over the promise and also that there should not
be unjust enrichment. It has to be borne in mind that since the
contractual breach is civil, the purpose behind the damages is to
compensate the injured party for the loss and not to punish the
aggrieved party. To determine the amount of damages, the
injured party has to show that the breach of contract caused him M/s YEW Consultancy Services Pvt. Ltd. Vs. M/s J.A. Brothers{CS(Comm) 314/2019} Page 59 of 77 financial losses and the principles of remoteness, causation and
mitigation apply to damages for breach of contract. The party
guilty of breach is liable only for reasonably foreseeable losses
that a prudent man possessing similar information during
contract would have reason to foresee the future breach.
11.13 The provision under Section 73 of the Indian
Contract Act provides 'loss or damages occurred in the usual
course of things in a contractual breach' as one of the important
requirements for awarding the damages. So, defendant is not
liable for the damages that are remote to the breach of contract.
In Hardley v. Baxendale (supra), the principle of remoteness was
described. The rule enumerated in this case was that the injured
party can only ask for the recovery of damages where the
damages are fairly and reasonably arising out naturally from the
usual course of things; or supposed to have been reasonably in
contemplation of both the parties at the time of the execution of
the contract, as its probable breach. Depending upon the facts
and circumstances, the courts have used several tests to establish
a causal link. The "But-for" test is one of the most important
tests used by the courts to determine whether the damages have
accrued 'but for' the acts of the defendants. 11.14 It may also be noted that the plaintiff must take all
reasonable steps to mitigate the loss which he has sustained
consequent upon the defendant's wrong, and, if he fails to do so,
he cannot claim damages for any such loss which he ought
reasonably to have avoided. Consequently, before seeking
damages, the obligation to mitigate losses is required and
plaintiff's effective mitigation measures are determined entirely
by the facts and circumstances of the case and in such M/s YEW Consultancy Services Pvt. Ltd. Vs. M/s J.A. Brothers{CS(Comm) 314/2019} Page 60 of 77 circumstances, the plaintiff has to ensure that he not only behaves
in his own best interests but also in the best interests of the
defendant, reducing the damages fairly. The purpose of the duty
to mitigate losses is relevant in the assessment of the damages
where the plaintiff is not entitled to any damages of losses which (s)he is supposed to have mitigated. Now I will refer to few
relevant precedent in the context. In Pannalal Jugatmal v. State
of Madhya Pradesh11 , it was observed that the mitigation of
damages is incorporated under Section 73 of the Contract Act.
The provision provides a burden on the party complaining about
the breach of the contract to show that he doesn't possess the
methods for remedying the damages caused to the non-
performance of the contract. In M. Lachia Setty & Sons Ltd. v.
Coffee Board, Bangalore12 , it was held by Hon'ble Supreme
Court that the principle of mitigation of loss doesn't give any
right to the party who is in breach of the contract but it is a
concept that has to be borne in the mind of the court while
awarding damages. 11.15 Determining the amount and computation of the
quantum of damages depends upon the magnitude of the injury
caused to the plaintiff for the contractual breach. Section 73 and 74 of the Indian Contract Act, only deal with the measures for
damages, but not the method for calculating the quantum of
damages incurred to the injured party. In, Murlidhar Chiranjilal
