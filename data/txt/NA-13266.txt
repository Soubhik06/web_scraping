The Karnataka High Court has asked the Union government to consider putting in place a system for digitally verifiable bank guarantees, after a case before it exposed how forged guarantees can pass undetected through existing tender processes. The observation came while the court was dealing with a dispute arising out of allegedly fake bank guarantees submitted during a railway tender, and the judge flagged the need for stronger, technology-backed verification mechanisms in public procurement.

The issue arose in two connected writ petitions filed under Article 226 of the Constitution by DRN Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd, challenging actions taken by the South Western Railway. Justice Suraj Govindaraj heard the connected petitions as a single-judge bench. Railway authorities terminated the bids after they found that the bank guarantees furnished during the bidding process were forged.

According to the judgement, the petitioner had furnished bank guarantees as part of the bidding process for railway works. The company claimed it had relied on third-party agents to arrange the guarantees and asserted that it only learnt of the fraud when the authorities raised concerns. It also pointed to a criminal complaint and chargesheet against the alleged fraudsters to demonstrate its lack of involvement.

Furthermore, while the court addressed the immediate contractual consequences in this case, it also treated the episode as symptomatic of a wider systemic vulnerability. Consequently, it recorded submissions from the Additional Solicitor General that the Ministry of Finance had taken note of the issue and observed that the Centre should examine the feasibility of introducing digitally verifiable bank guarantees to prevent similar frauds in the future.

The court suggested that banks could issue guarantees in a digitally verifiable form, incorporating secure and tamper-proof QR codes or similar authentication tools. Such mechanisms, the court noted, could allow instant verification of key details, including the issuing bank and branch, beneficiary, amount, validity period, and subsisting status, directly from the issuer’s system.

In addition, the court said authorities may consider adopting a centralised or interoperable digital verification platform. They further said authorities could make such a platform accessible to procuring authorities, public sector undertakings, and other beneficiary institutions, enabling them to verify guarantees directly with issuing banks.

The judgement further pointed to the feasibility of secured electronic interfaces or application programming interfaces (APIs) that would enable real-time or near real-time confirmation of bank guarantees between issuing banks and beneficiary authorities. Such interfaces, the court observed, could operate at critical stages such as tender evaluation, award, or contract administration, thereby reducing discretion, delays, and the scope for manipulation.

Moreover, the court suggested assigning a unique, non-reusable identification number to each bank guarantee. This identifier, capable of verification throughout the guarantee’s lifecycle, from issuance and amendment to extension, invocation, and discharge, could help prevent duplication, alteration, or reuse of fraudulent instruments.

The court also proposed examining a transition towards digital-only issuance of bank guarantees, supported by bank-grade encryption, digital signatures, and time-stamped issuance records, while treating any physical copies as non-authoritative representations. For higher-value guarantees, it suggested requiring direct issuer-side confirmation to beneficiary authorities through secure electronic communication as an additional safeguard.

Finally, it highlighted the potential benefits of standardising minimum data fields and formats across banks and maintaining an auditable electronic trail of verification attempts to enhance transparency, accountability, and oversight.

The bench classified forged bank guarantees as a “serious and recurring concern”. Recording submissions made on behalf of the Union government, the court noted that nearly 12 other entities “have been cheated” through fabricated guarantees that were acted upon as genuine, resulting in “substantial financial exposure and avoidable contractual and legal disputes”. Consequently, the court treated the episode as indicative of a wider failure in existing tender verification systems.

Moreover, the court expressly observed that the problem “is not confined to the facts of the present case alone” but instead reveals a “systemic vulnerability in the existing processes governing the submission, acceptance, and verification of bank guarantees”. In particular, it warned that the absence of a uniform and secure authentication mechanism “creates fertile ground for fraud”, while simultaneously undermining commercial certainty, posing risks to institutional trust in public procurement.

Importantly, the court framed the issue in terms of public interest rather than private loss. It stressed that bank guarantees “occupy a position of central importance in commercial and governmental transactions”. It cautioned that any erosion of confidence in their authenticity carries consequences that extend well beyond individual tenders. Such failures, the court said, directly implicate “public interest, fiscal discipline, and the integrity of public procurement processes”.

Finally, the court warned that failing to address repeated instances of fraudulent bank guarantees at a systemic level could cause “recurring financial loss, multiplicity of litigation, and diminished confidence in tendering frameworks administered by the State”.
