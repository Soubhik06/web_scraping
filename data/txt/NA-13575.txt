By Zoya Vickers

Social media and teens. Perhaps the two most controversial topics worldwide. So when these two, clubbed with law-making, take the front page, it’s no surprise that everyone has their two cents to add to the discussion, and these are from the other side, the teens, or rather, banned. The recent passage of Australia’s Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Bill 2024 on November 29, 2024, has dominated headlines and divided public opinion. Those in support of the ban hail it as a necessary step to protect children, while critics denounce it as an infringement on free speech, further isolating teens from vital sources of news, connection, and opportunity.

The Bill imposes a blanket ban on social media usage for individuals under the age of 16 and places the responsibility of its enforcement on social media companies. Platforms have been given a year to comply, with fines of up to USD 32.5 million for non-compliance. Since its announcement, the legislation has drawn both support and criticism, reflecting the complexity of balancing online safety with the realities of digital engagement.

The primary aim of the Bill is to safeguard children from the harmful effects of social media, including cyberbullying, body image issues, and exposure to inappropriate content. As stated by Australian Prime Minister, Anthony Albanese in the ‘Social media reforms to protect our kids online pass Parliament’ press release , dated Friday 29 November 2024:

“We’ve passed important legislation to keep our kids safe online.”

“Social media is doing social harm to our kids. We’ve called time on it.”

“We want our kids to have a childhood and parents to know we have their backs.”

While these statements highlight the government’s protective stance, they remain vague, leaving questions about how the law will achieve its objectives in practice.

The idea of banning social media may seem fruitful in a perfect world, but not when one considers the other aspects and factors that may circumvent or nullify the purpose of the ban. Critics argue that the bill oversimplifies the complexities of online safety and could lead to unintended consequences that undermine its purpose. Several concerns have been raised, such as banning children from mainstream social media could push them toward less regulated, potentially more dangerous platforms. These alternative spaces often lack moderation, exposing users to greater risks such as exploitation, cyberbullying, and exposure to harmful content. Instead of addressing the root causes of harm, the ban may shift the problem elsewhere.

We need to remember that for many children in unsafe environments, social media isn’t just entertainment; many rely on it for vital support, including safe spaces to access mental health resources and support groups. Denying them this connection could leave them isolated and more vulnerable.

Social media is not solely a space for frivolity. Many teens use platforms like Instagram and TikTok to showcase their talents, such as art, music, or writing, and to connect with opportunities that might otherwise be inaccessible. For example, young artists may gain commissions, and aspiring performers can build audiences that propel their careers. Moreover, platforms serve as hubs for learning and development. Many teens follow pages offering free advice on college applications, scholarships, and career paths, providing them with resources that might be unaffordable or unavailable to them in any other way.

Banning a child’s account may prevent them from accessing social media independently, but what about ‘family bloggers’ or ‘mommy bloggers’ who profit from the appearance of young children, from infants to teenagers, in their videos, often forcing them into uncomfortable situations, or recording without consent? Surely this is a larger threat to these children ‘having a childhood’? While a teen’s own account may be used to post only about what they wish to share, in cases like the above-mentioned ones, children are turned into public spectacles for monetary gain. This means they have no agency over their online presence and are subjected to permanent digital footprints created by their parents. In her paper, Family Vlogging And Child Harm: A Need For Nationwide Protection , Camille Laude stresses the abuse and monetisation of children through platforms like YouTube and promotes the intervention of the law to protect the interests of these children. Growing up in front of a camera: The worrying trend of family influencers documenting every second of their children’s upbringings , penned by Ariana Dell brings up the case of Ruby Franke, who abused her children by denying them meals or the ‘right’ to sleep on a bed.

Is banning teens from creating their own social media accounts truly more urgent than addressing this widespread exploitation? If the goal is to protect children’s childhoods, issues like these must be addressed and given greater importance. Investigating the Ethical Implications of Family Vlogging and Sharenting by Joan McEnaney.

The Australian ban is akin to disallowing children to visit beaches and seas because ‘a shark may eat them.’ Is this truly the mentality with which such issues should be addressed? Experts have long advocated for education as a more effective alternative to outright bans. Teaching children and teens about the dangers of social media, how to tackle it and basic digital literacy, empowers the future generation to be able to navigate the digital world instead of being thrown into the deep end as soon as they are over sixteen. Examples of this are the UK’s “Media Smart” Programme and “ Ctrl+Alt+Delete” by MediaSmarts , which focused on identifying problems, treating teens as partners trying to understand and work together, instead of something to be controlled and restricted. This made the approach more successful, as root problems were easily identified and children were more receptive to the initiative.

Teens don’t like being told what to do. Or, at least that’s what media and adults tend to perpetuate. This statement is very selective in its recognition of teens’ behaviours. Simply put, children, teenagers, and pretty much anyone don’t enjoy being treated like something to be controlled, or denied the chance to have their say about things that concern them. Or to be told what to do without being told why, or being allowed to question it. By simply involving them in the conversation, it increases impact, efficiency and reach. Pretending that children don’t know anything about the internet and how to protect themselves, or that adults have never fallen prey to scams or harassment, isn’t doing anyone any favours, it’s only making an already complex issue more and more messy and confusing. Instead, we must understand, it isn’t Adults vs Teens, it’s Children, Parents, and anyone else who wants to make the internet a safer place vs those who wish to exploit us.

Enforcing the bill is another issue being discussed by many specialists. Social media companies will rely on age-verification systems to comply with the law. However, these systems are notoriously unreliable, and many teens are adept at bypassing such restrictions using incorrect information or fake accounts. Doing so isn’t hard, as many websites rely on user-provided information. Children often use throwaway accounts to access apps or media by feeding in incorrect dates of birth or using throwaway Gmail accounts. More often than not, these methods are used when teens don’t trust the websites or apps and don’t feel comfortable providing them with information and data about themselves.

This is where a new kind of verification comes into play. This includes language analysis (Snapchat, TikTok and Twitch), data categorisation (YouTube), AI tools that estimate age based on facial features (TikTok and Twitch), etc. However, these are dicey at best. Age estimation is often inaccurate and discriminatory, especially to people of colour , as well as having a large margin of error extending up to four years, which means an adult may be mistaken for a child and restricted from platforms, whereas an older teen may be perfectly capable of bypassing the system.

Aside from those already in practice, the eSafety Commissioner has stated: “Proactive tools and technologies may rely on a user actively engaging with a service (such as connecting with others, communicating with others, sharing and creating content) to detect relevant signals.” This demonstrates the invasive nature of such verification. While the Australian government has mandated the destruction of this information after its intended purpose has been served, such action remains questionable.

Additionally, privacy advocates have raised alarms over this issue, arguing that they pave the way to perpetuate these intrusive data collection practices. Critics have questioned the technicalities of these systems, claiming they prioritise surveillance over safety and could prove an obstacle when taking into account the need for privacy and anonymity online. With many of these tech giants (TikTok, Facebook) already guilty of trying to nudge users towards invasive privacy policies , they may take advantage of an opportunity handed to them by the Australian government to do the same. This can stifle children’s creativity, limit their participation in online communities, and prevent them from seeking support or information on sensitive topics.

The Australian Government has washed its hands of the responsibility to educate, prepare and teach its children about how to navigate the internet and their usage of social media. After passing this blanket ban, the government has stated that ‘reasonable steps’ must be taken by the concerned platforms, without much elaboration on what such ‘reasonable steps’ might be. Furthermore, the enforcement of this bill has been made the responsibility of the social media apps, while the eSafety Commissioner is tasked with formulating guidelines to clarify what constitutes these steps, and while recommendations are being made, they remain controversial at best.

While the need for responsible use of social media and the protection of children on these platforms is necessary, the extent of the restrictions must be deliberated upon. We must recognise that complex issues cannot be tackled by simply shutting down every aspect, but should instead be carefully considered and be addressed with responses and methods which, at the very least, try to cater to every detail and contradiction. Australia has spearheaded a movement to protect its future generation, but while its sentiment is admirable, the many flaws and inconsistencies must be addressed to ensure the ban doesn’t inadvertently do more harm than good.
