The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) released a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for handling cases of non-consensual intimate images (NCII) in November, following a directive from the Madras High Court . The SOP outlines how victims can report such content, the roles of social media platforms and government agencies, and timelines for takedowns and coordination with law enforcement.

Around the same time, MeitY also proposed draft amendments to the IT Rules to regulate synthetically generated information, including deepfakes, through labelling and disclosure requirements. Together, these measures signal the governmentâs attempt to address the growing scale of online sexual abuse and AI-enabled image manipulation.

However, questions remain around how these frameworks work in practice, whether responsibilities are being disproportionately placed on platforms, and where gaps persist in implementation and survivor support.

To understand these issues and the challenges on the ground, MediaNama spoke with Siddharth Pillai, co-founder of the RATI Foundation, which runs Meri Trustline, a support service for victims of online sexual harm in India.

MediaNama The SOP that MeitY released is one of the first SOPs that specifically addresses non-consensual intimate imagery. Do you think the definition of NCII is adequate, and does it cover all forms of NCII?

Siddharth Pillai, RATI The focus is on the file, the content itself, and how explicit it is. Recently, we had a case of a girl who wanted a photo taken down simply because she was wearing jeans and someone had uploaded it out of context. She is from a very remote area where women do not wear jeans.

Now, such content, which forms the majority of cases, such as a man and a woman holding hands, sitting on a bike, or sitting in a hotel room, also qualifies as NCII content, though it may be non-severe in nature. However, such content falls outside the ambit of these rules and is not being classified as NCII under these circulars.

Another issue is the devaluation of consent. If you look at any gender-violence law in India, consent is central. An image of me wearing jeans may not be explicit at all, but if it is leaked without my consent, it becomes violence. This has been a long struggle in gender law. While earlier laws centred consent, recent laws at the intersection of gender and technology appear to dilute it.

MediaNama The SOP definition specifically mentions female victims. Does this risk excluding other genders from accessing justice or takedowns under this SOP?

Siddharth Pillai Yes, definitely. That is another shortcoming. Based on helpline calls we receive, particularly related to NCII, around 20% of complainants are male. A significant proportion also identify as other genders. The SOP does not adequately capture the full gender spectrum or recognise who is at harm.

MediaNama The SOP focuses primarily on how victims can get NCII removed from the internet. What should also be considered in terms of how government agencies function? Should there be a separate SOP on how complaints of NCII or online sexual harm are handled? What shortcomings have you seen when victims approach government agencies?

Siddharth Pillai, RATI Government communication, including this circular, suggests that victims should go to the police or a government-approved one-stop crisis centre and relief will follow. In practice, this is not how the system works. Police stations often struggle to register FIRs when the perpetrator is abroad, and victims may be discouraged, or their complaints never converted into FIRs.

Victims often have to spend an entire day at a police station. Evidence requirements can go beyond screenshots, sometimes requiring submission of the victimâs phone, which then becomes evidence for years and may not be usable when returned. This reality is not conveyed to victims beforehand. The SOP makes the process appear simple, listing five bodies that will assist, but the experience on the ground is far more complex.

One-stop crisis centres have been introduced without sufficient capacity building. Based on our experience, many are not equipped to handle online abuse cases. Police stations also process evidence inconsistently. We have received calls where officers asked victims to send evidence to their personal WhatsApp numbers due to lack of clarity. This raises serious privacy and security concerns. Overall, while the SOP reflects good intent, it leaves many practical issues unaddressed.

MediaNama The SOP mentions takedowns from social media platforms and de-indexing of URLs. What happens when NCII is circulated through closed WhatsApp groups or other private messaging platforms?

Siddharth Pillai, RATI We need to be realistic. You cannot promise that all content will disappear from the internet. Perpetrators may store it offline. Many circulars apply only to significant intermediaries with over 50,000 Indian users, leaving out many platforms.

De-indexing does not remove content at the source; it only adds another layer of protection. That is still useful, because online security works in layers. However, this SOP relies too heavily on de-indexing and URL-level blocking and does not take a long-term view.

Victims need more than takedowns. They need mental health support and help addressing the social impact of content leaks. India already has a relatively strong coercive takedown mechanism compared to other countries, so this aspect has been addressed to a large extent.

MediaNama On the Google Play Store, several apps can generate AI videos of people kissing using uploaded photos. These are not nude but still intimate. How does the government address the creation and spread of such content?

Siddharth Pillai, RATI Intent must be central. In gender-based violence, consent determines harm. Hugging someone is fine; hugging them without consent is a violation. The same applies here. De-indexing is only one layer and is easily circumvented.

If the goal is meaningful redress without over-regulation, consent and intent must guide policy. The harm caused is not technical or about copyright; it is about dignity. These harms cannot always be measured technically, which is why consent and intent are essential indicators, including for AI-generated content.

MediaNama When it comes to access to justice, how significant are gender, age, class, and regional divides? Will the NCII SOP adequately address these?

Siddharth Pillai, RATI Many reporting mechanisms, including Government grievance or complaint (GAC) form, are available only in English. This creates a clear digital and linguistic divide. There is no requirement in the SOP to provide translations. People with interrupted or limited literacy often rely on voice-based phone use, how do they access these systems? Accessibility for persons with disabilities also remains unclear.

Even for educated and privileged users, filling a GAC form without legal assistance is difficult. There is substantial work needed to make these systems truly accessible.

Biases also emerge at police stations. Research we conducted in 2020 showed that younger victims are treated with more trust, while older women often face greater suspicion. The severity of content is also over-emphasised. Perpetrators adapt by reducing explicitness while maintaining the same level of harm. A blurred image can still deeply violate a victim, even if others do not recognise it as explicit. These nuances are not captured in the SOP.

MediaNama Over the next five years, what concrete policy changes would you like to see to make responses to online sexual harm and NCII more victim-centric?

Siddharth Pillai, RATI Access must be easier, across language, treatment, and sensitivity. Standards prescribed in gender laws must apply equally to technology-facilitated harm. We have the provisions and techniques, but they are not implemented effectively.

Platforms should publish transparent standards of evidence so victims know what is required and what outcomes to expect. Police procedures for evidence collection must be standardised through a clear SOP.

Platforms should also broaden protected categories. For example, children of sex workers face significant online harassment but are not recognised as a protected group. Policies must reflect local contexts and identities. Finally, the entire process, from takedowns to justice, needs to be significantly faster.
