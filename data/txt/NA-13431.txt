Reserve Bank of India (RBI) Governor Sanjay Malhotra, on January 9, 2026, said that India needs technology-driven, near real-time supervision of the digital financial system.

Speaking at the Third Annual Global Conference of the College of Supervisors in Mumbai, Malhotra said that technology has reshaped products, partnerships, and processes across the financial system, widening access and enhancing efficiency, but also creating new risks.

He remarked that banking regulation and supervision framework must adapt faster to the pace and scale of digital transformation, while keeping systemic stability and consumer protection at its core.

At the same time, he said regulators and regulated entities must work as partners, not adversaries, in order to build systemic resilience. Malhotra also stressed that supervisory action by the RBI focuses on correction rather than punishment, and that better use of regulatory data and analytics is essential for early risk detection.

However, he warned that digitalisation must remain customer-centric, with stronger safeguards against rising digital fraud. Malhotra added that capacity building across banks and regulators will be critical as technology continues to redefine financial supervision.

The RBI governor set out a clear push towards technology-led, near real-time supervision, arguing that traditional periodic and on-site models are no longer sufficient for a fast-moving digital financial system.

He said the RBI is already collecting large volumes of data through platforms such as the Centralised Information Management System (CIMS) and Advanced Supervisory Monitoring System (DAKSH), and that the introduction of the Supervisory Data Quality Index (SDQI) last year has improved reporting standards.

Furthermore, Malhotra said that the Department of Supervision should build “stronger analytics and supervisory dashboards for enhanced off-site surveillance”, so that risks can be identified early rather than after damage has already occurred. Consequently, he said supervision must become “more off-site than on-site and as near real-time [as possible]”.

And in this context, he signalled a decisive move towards greater use of supervisory technology. “Increasingly, this will also mean using SupTech (supervisory technology) and AI-enabled tools more deeply,” Malhotra said. However, he stressed that automation cannot replace regulatory responsibility. Instead, technology must strengthen oversight while keeping decision-making firmly with human supervisors.

Crucially, Malhotra warned against the idea that digital tools should weaken regulatory discipline. “Technology must embed compliance, not bypass it,” he said. Malhotra added that, “Accountability must remain human, and automation should not dilute accountability — it should sharpen it.”

The RBI governor also outlined a more nuanced approach to enforcement, placing it within a broader supervisory continuum rather than treating it as a standalone response. He said that supervisory action should begin with training, guidance and dialogue, and only escalate to penalties when other tools fail. “Enforcement, restrictions and penalties are measures of last resort”, he said, adding that the RBI’s intent is “largely to correct” rather than to punish.

Accordingly, Malhotra said enforcement serves two purposes: to signal unacceptable conduct to the institution concerned and, equally, to communicate regulatory expectations to the wider market. Therefore, he argued, supervision should encourage self-correction, with enforcement acting only as a backstop in a well-functioning financial ecosystem.

At the same time, he placed strong emphasis on consumer protection in a digital-first banking system. He warned that while digital channels improve access and convenience, they can also facilitate “opaque pricing, weak disclosures and inappropriate recovery practices” if left unchecked. As a result, he said customer protection must become “the cornerstone of a sustainable and resilient financial system”.

Malhotra also flagged digital fraud as an urgent systemic risk that requires collective action. While banks must strengthen their own controls, he said the sector must work together to build shared analytics to detect mule accounts and suspicious transactions “timely and pre-emptively”.

Finally, he said these changes demand continuous capacity building across regulators and institutions. Supervisors and regulated entities, he said, must learn from each other, develop a shared language of oversight, and move beyond a “tick-box based compliance culture”, towards a common understanding that can reduce friction and improve outcomes.
