Last week, an IIT Madras-incubated firm JandK Operations launched BharOS, claiming it to be India’s indigenous (or Atmanirbhar) smartphone operating system. Then, earlier this week, the buzz around it grew when two Indian ministers participated in a video call using smartphones running BharOS.Â Since then, multiple government bodies have tweeted about it and the media has covered it extensively, pitching itÂ as India’s answer to Android and iOS.Â But does BharOS really merit this attention? Here’s why I don’t think it does:

1. BharOS is an Android fork: Firstly, the “indigenous” OS is not really all that indigenous as it appears to be based on Android’s Open Source Project (AOSP) . Using AOSP, anyone and everyone can build custom variants of Android. These versions are usually referred to as Android forks. BharOS is one such Android fork. There are many others like this, such as Amazon’s Fire OS and Xiaomi’s MIUI. What makes forks different from Google’s Android (aka stock Android) is that they don’t come with Play Service or Google apps installed by default. There is nothing inherently wrong with being a forkâenough customisations can make you stand out significantly from the stock Android. There are forks like Graphene OS which offer more security and privacy features than stock Android. The issue with BharOS is that its makers don’t acknowledge its Android roots and portray it to be something built from the ground up. Here’s an interesting thread on this:

Here we have yet another case study of a totally independently developed OS that's actually just AOSP.

2. Consumers have nothing to gain: If you’re an Android user, have you ever felt that the OS is lacking and something better needs to come up? Likely not. For most users, Android provides a great experience already. You don’t have to worry about security as updates are issued regularly, you don’t have to worry about fraudulent apps as Play Store filters most of them, you don’t have to worry about having the basic apps like search, maps, and web browser because Google’s versions of these are pre-installed,Â you don’t have to worry about finding the right app to do something because Play Store has millions of apps to choose from, etc. With BharOS, users don’t get any of these. They do, however, get to choose what apps go on their devices. But what if they want Play Store because it’s currently the best app store? They cannot get it because BharOS doesn’t have a license for it. Instead, consumers will have to settle for an inferior app store, which might not even have all the apps they’re looking for.

3. Benefits to developers are not clear:Â Okay, let’s set aside the consumers. The primary issue with Android is that Google exercises significant control over it and preinstalls its own apps. Additionally, it takes a 30 percent commission for in-app purchases for apps downloaded from Play Store. These are major problems for developers. A superior maps app might not get downloaded because Google Maps is already on the phone. Or a highly popular gaming app might have to share 30 percent of its hard-earned revenue with Google and if it doesn’t, it will be pulled from the Play Store. But does BharOS solve these issues? Since it doesn’t come with pre-installed apps, it might cause users to download non-Google apps (although users might prefer Google’s apps because of familiarity), but from where? The developers of BharOS said that the OS will “provides access to trusted apps from organisation-specific Private App Store Services (PASS). A PASS provides access to a curated list of apps that have been thoroughly vetted and have met certain security and privacy standards of organisations.”

4. NoÂ clarity onÂ security and updates:Â For all its faults, Google ensures one thing: Android gets security updates regularly. This is very crucial for users because an outdated OS is an easy target for hackers and for cybercrimes. BharOS developers noted that its devices will get “Native Over The Air (NOTA) updates that can help to keep the devices secure. NOTA updates are automatically downloaded and installed on the device, without the need for the user to manually initiate the process.” But there is no clarity on how long devices will get updates and how fast will updates be rolled out by 1) BharOS and 2) smartphone manufacturers. Here is a thread on more such security concerns, raised by Android expert Mishaal Raman:

If installing it requires unlocking the bootloader & the phones they support don't support flashing custom AVB keys, then that's insecure by design. You need to be able to lock the bootloader to ensure critical partitions can't be tampered with.

“BharOS Services are currently being provided to organisations that have stringent privacy and security requirements whose users handle sensitive information that requires confidential communications on restricted apps on mobiles,” the press release from the developer states, but there is no information on how BharOS will guarantee privacy and security.

5.Â In light of the CCI orders:Â The Competition Commission of India (CCI) in October 2022 issued antitrust orders requiring Google to make extensive changes to Android and Play Store . Google on January 25, agreed to make some of these changes. Google is expected to make all of the prescribed changes, and if that happens, won’t antitrust concerns be addressed by the CCI orders? What’s the need for a BharOS then?
