The CUTS International and IIIT Bangalore report on Multi-Party Privacy (MPP) within India’s digital credit sector highlights the intricate challenges and significant awareness gaps surrounding the privacy of co-owned data among consumers. The study employs a multi-method approach, including controlled experiments, focus group discussions, surveys, and stakeholder interviews, to investigate privacy behaviours, perceptions, and trade-offs consumers face in digital lending scenarios.

The report reveals that a majority of digital financial service (DFS) users are neutral about privacy protection, often prioritising factors like convenience and financial incentives over personal data security. Only a quarter of users proactively protect their personal information. Awareness of MPP, especially regarding co-owned data in digital financial services, is low among surveyed users. Many are unaware of the shared ownership aspect of their data, leading to a lack of informed decision-making. This lack of privacy awareness contributes to a “choicelessness” in data sharing, where consumers have limited options to manage or protect co-owned data.

Out of the 2,178 surveyed in the report, 461 respondents (approximately 21%) were digital borrowers using digital credit facilities. The remaining only used DFS for payments and receipts.

Consumers often share personal data when the provider is deemed reliable, indicating a readiness to trade privacy for perceived safety. The report emphasizes that digital lenders increasingly use alternative data—such as social media activity and transaction histories—to assess creditworthiness, often without explicit consumer consent. This practice raises significant privacy concerns as co-owned data involves multiple parties. Individuals may be unaware or unable to control how their information is shared.

MPP refers to the privacy concerns and challenges that arise when personal data is shared among multiple stakeholders or parties. This concept is particularly relevant in contexts where data ownership is not singular. Information may be co-owned or jointly shared by different individuals or entities. MPP is significant in various domains, including social networks, digital finance, and collaborative platforms, where data sharing is common.

MediaNama talked to Amol Kulkarni, Director (Research), CUTS International regarding multi-party privacy concerns amongst consumers of digital credit in India.

Amol Kulkarni, suggests several measures consumers can adopt to safeguard their privacy when using digital lending applications. He advises using only genuine applications, being cautious of “offers that seem too good to be true,” and thoroughly checking “comments and ratings of applications” to gauge their authenticity. Kulkarni further emphasises the importance of avoiding impersonators by “carefully checking logos” and managing app permissions wisely. He recommends allowing data access only when necessary, using one-time access if possible, and refraining from granting access to the gallery or social media accounts. Kulkarni also advises consumers to “be thoughtful while sharing data which has sensitive information about others as well” and suggests informing others if their data might be shared in association with the user.

Kulkarni points out that the RBI has restricted the collection of irrelevant data in digital lending and issued guidelines to ensure privacy and data protection. However, Kulkarni highlights ongoing compliance issues, with numerous fake loan apps still collecting and misusing consumer data. He explains, “The typical regulatory responses have been to ban such apps. However, the players reappear by new names and continue cheating consumers and committing fraud.”

Cultural perspectives on privacy, digital interactions, and finance strongly influence consumer behaviour in digital lending. Kulkarni states that in India, lending is often perceived as an act of help, which can inadvertently place lenders on a pedestal. This attitude extends to digital lending, where consumers may assume lenders need access to extensive personal data. However, Kulkarni asserts, “just because somebody is lending you money digitally, that doesn’t mean that such person needs access to all kinds of data.” He emphasises that while some data trade-offs are necessary, consumers should make informed decisions and share only essential data.

Aadhaar and other ID documents are widely accepted as necessary for verification in digital lending, leading many consumers to share them without question. Kulkarni observes that consumers “don’t often question why sharing of such documents is required,” even online, where they might upload scans of IDs without a second thought. In traditional lending, a guarantor role often involves providing relatives’ contact details; in digital lending, this expectation translates to access permissions for contact lists, potentially allowing lenders to contact these individuals in case of repayment delays. Consumers may feel compelled to provide such access to obtain loans, despite privacy concerns, Kulkarni notes.

Demographic factors influence privacy perceptions and responses to privacy risks in digital lending. Kulkarni explains that different groups display “differential sensitivity” to privacy. For instance, women may be more protective of personal photographs, stated Kulkarni. Many consumers report feeling  “choicelessness,” where they believe they must provide extensive data permissions to access services. According to Kulkarni, consumers lack the time or technological assistance to make informed decisions about data sharing, creating a sense of helplessness.

Kulkarni states that current regulations rely on consent from the primary data holder, which often fails to account for multi-party privacy, as consent is generally not sought from other affected individuals. Kulkarni adds that “taking consent or at least informing all persons to whom the data belongs may not be technologically or practically feasible” in the present framework.
